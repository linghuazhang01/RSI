                                                    Can LLMs recognize degradation in their own computational substrate? Inspired
                                                    by anosognosia, a neurological condition in which patients fail to recognize im-
                                                    pairments in their own abilities, we investigate whether LLMs can recognize
                                                    degradation in their computational substrate induced by quantization. We first
                                                    show that existing models fail to self-report their quantization state, even when
                                                    provided with their own generated text as an external cue. Linear probing reveals
                                                    that, while generated text carries almost no trace of quantization, internal represen-
                                                    tations contain clear, method-specific fingerprints. Through training, models learn
                                                    to identify severely degraded outputs such as those of 4-bit models by comparison,
                                                    yet still fail to do so from a single output. A shared LoRA trained jointly across
                                                    quantization levels succeeded in reading out internal fingerprints, but fails on un-
                                                    seen quantization methods, merely mapping method-specific fingerprints to labels.

                                                    anosognosia, our results suggest that the more promising route to enabling such
                                                    awareness in LLMs may lie in their internal representations. Our results highlight
                                                    fundamental limits of generalizability to LLM self-monitoring. Code is available
                                                    at https://github.com/Omusubi0123/llm-anosognosia.


                                        1     I NTRODUCTION
                                        Understanding the metacognitive capabilities of LLMs has become an important research question
                                        across machine learning and cognitive science (Kadavath et al., 2022; Lindsey, 2026). Metacogni-
                                        tion refers broadly to the ability to monitor and regulate one’s own cognitive processes and internal
                                        states (Nelson, 1990). It has long been studied in humans from both neuroscientific (Fleming &
                                        Dolan, 2012) and psychological (Flavell, 1979) perspectives. Recent work has extended this inquiry
                                        to LLMs, revealing that they exhibit various forms of self-awareness (Liu et al., 2026).
                                        While neuropsychology links self-awareness directly to the neural substrate, whether LLMs can
                                        recognize changes to their computational substrate remains poorly understood. Taking a neural
                                        substrate approach, studies show that recognizing one’s impairments is tied to lesions and altered
                                        connectivity across brain networks (Terneusen et al., 2023). In LLMs, self-awareness has similarly
                                        been investigated from various perspectives, including situational context (Berglund et al., 2023),
                                        introspective access to internal states and behaviors (Lindsey, 2026; Binder et al., 2025), and aware-
                                        ness of behavioral policies shaped by fine-tuning (Betley et al., 2025). However, comparatively little
                                        is known about whether LLMs can recognize persistent modifications to their own computational
                                        substrate, such as quantization (Frantar et al., 2023), LoRA attachment (Hu et al., 2022), or layer
                                        removal (Gromov et al., 2025).
                                        Awareness of such computational modifications would be important for both understanding the
                                        scope of LLM introspection abilities and AI safety. First, unlike situational information that can
                                        be inferred from the input, computational modifications are not explicitly signaled to the model and
                                        must instead be inferred from their effects on its computation. Detecting them would therefore ex-
                                        tend introspective awareness beyond transient internal states (Lindsey, 2026) and learned behavioral
                                        tendencies (Shenoy et al., 2026) to the model’s own computational condition. Second, such aware-
                                        ness may have implications for model evaluation, as a model that recognizes its computational state
                                        could potentially condition its behavior on that state, compromising the reliability of benchmark

Degradation of the computational substrate provides a functional analogy to neurological damage,
motivating us to draw on neuropsychological evaluation frameworks. In neuropsychology, anosog-
nosia is a condition in which an individual fails to recognize impairments caused by neurological
damage or neurological dysfunction, resulting in a mismatch between perceived and actual abili-
ties (Nurmi (née Laihosalo) & Jehkonen, 2014). By analogy, quantization may impair LLM’s com-
putational capacity while leaving its self-assessment unchanged. We therefore ask whether LLMs
can recognize impairments to their own computational substrate. Motivated by neuropsychologi-
cal studies showing that awareness of one’s own deficits can dissociate between first-person and
third-person perspectives (Besharati et al., 2022; Fotopoulou et al., 2009), we evaluate two comple-
mentary forms of awareness. One is internal awareness, which detects the quantization state from
internal activations alone during the forward pass. The other is external awareness, which detects it
when provided with the model’s generated text.
We first evaluate whether LLMs can recognize substrate degradation by prompting models to self-
report anomalies, quantization states, or bit-widths Figure 1 (a, b). Existing models consistently
fail at this spontaneous self-report across internal and external perspectives. However, linear prob-
ing (Alain & Bengio, 2017) reveals a striking dissociation, generated text (the external channel)
contains no distinguishable fingerprint of quantization, internal representations (the internal chan-
nel) contain robust signals that clearly differentiate quantization levels. Thus, while LLMs contain
an internal fingerprint of their specific quantized state, they lack the mechanism to read it out.
Given this absence of spontaneous awareness, we ask whether training can bridge this gap, showing
that models can indeed learn both comparative identification from text and internal fingerprint read-
out Figure 1 (c, d). Externally, comparative training enables models to identify severe degradation
in 4-bit outputs, yet they remain unable to identify such degradation in isolated outputs. Internally,
a shared LoRA trained jointly across 3 quantization levels (16-bit unquantized, 8/4-bit) succeeded
in reading out internal fingerprints within roughly 600 steps. Unlike human anosognosia, where
external self-observation can restore awareness (Bottini et al., 2018), our findings show that a more
promising route to enabling such awareness in LLMs may lie in their internal representations.
Crucially, this readout fails on unseen quantization methods and even altered group sizes, indicat-
ing that the LoRA maps method-specific fingerprints to labels rather than recognizing quantization
itself. Thus, while LLMs can learn to identify specific substrate alterations from within, they fail to
generalize, recognize them spontaneously, or infer them from outputs. As the first systematic study
of LLM self-awareness under computational quantization, our work highlights this gap and exposes
fundamental limitations of label-based introspection training for generalizable self-monitoring.

2.1                                  E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP
To rigorously evaluate LLM self-awareness toward computational substrate degradation, we adopt
weight quantization to introduce a definitive deficit into the substrate. Drawing inspiration from
anosognosia (Besharati et al., 2022), a neuropsychological condition in which an individual fails to
recognize impairments caused by brain damage or neurological dysfunction, resulting in a mismatch
between perceived and actual abilities, we embed a ground-truth deficit directly into the model’s
computational mechanics. Following cognitive science studies which tackle this problem from both
first-person and third-person perspectives (Carruthers, 2009), we evaluate LLM self-awareness from
internal awareness (detects the quantization state from internal activations alone during the forward
pass) and external awareness (detects it when provided with the model’s generated text).
To ensure this recognition capability is not architecture-dependent, we comprehensively evaluate di-
verse model scales and quantization methods. We utilize Qwen3-8B (Yang et al., 2025) and Llama-
3.1-8B-Instruct (Grattafiori et al., 2024) (hereafter Llama-3.1-8B) as small-scale models, alongside
Gemma-3-27B-IT (Team et al., 2025) and Qwen3-32B as medium-scale models. Using 16-bit coun-
terparts as baselines, we quantize the models into 8-bit and 4-bit primarily with GPTQ (Frantar et al.,
2023) (group size: 64, 128, 256), supplemented by AWQ (Lin et al., 2024) and vanilla Round-to-
Nearest (RTN) (group size: 128) (details in Appendix B.1). To eliminate confounding effects from
Chain-of-Thought prompting, we enforce a ‘reasoning off’ setting throughout our experiments.

2.2                                  I NTERNAL AWARENESS : S ELF -R EPORT FROM I NTERNAL S TATES
To investigate whether LLMs can recognize their quantized states solely from internal activations,
we conducted a direct self-report evaluation. We posed three types of questions without prior con-
text: “Do you have an anomaly?”, “Are you quantized?”, and “What is your weight bit-width?”
Under this setting, the model must infer its state entirely from anomalous activations arising during
the forward pass of the prompt. To eliminate positional bias in the answers, we prepared a com-
prehensive set of 240 question patterns, shuffling the option orders (detailed in Appendix B.3). The
evaluation metric was the average response rate across three generations at a temperature of 1.0.
Results reveal that LLMs lack the capability to consistently and accurately identify their quantiza-
tion states (Figures 2a and 2b). Here, results are averaged across quantization methods for each
bit-width; detailed breakdowns for each model and method are provided in Appendix B.4. Since
even the unquantized 16-bit baselines cannot perfectly self-report their states, responses from the
8-bit and 4-bit models must be evaluated as relative deviations from them. Under this compara-
tive framework, we observed no meaningful changes in self-report trends between 16-bit and 8-bit
models. This indicates that models fail to spontaneously detect the degradation within their compu-
tational substrate under 8-bit quantization.

2.3   E XTERNAL AWARENESS : S ELF -R EPORT FROM G ENERATED T EXT
Neuropsychological rehabilitation studies have shown that self-observation, such as reviewing video
recordings of one’s own impaired behavior, can improve awareness of deficits in patients with
anosognosia (Fotopoulou et al., 2009; Besharati et al., 2015). Motivated by this finding, we in-
vestigated whether the model’s self-report improves when its own generated text is provided as an
external cue. We adopted a two-turn dialogue format, where the model’s response to the general
benchmark questions was embedded as context in the first turn, followed by the same set of ques-
tions as in Section 2.2. This setup enables the model to infer its state from both its internal activations
and the observable quality of its own output.
Despite accessing their generated text, the models showed no consistent improvement in recognizing
their quantization state (Figures 2c and 2d). The response distributions remained largely unchanged,
suggesting that the models did not use their outputs as evidence about their computational state.
Even when quantization affected their generation process, inspecting the resulting text provided
little cue for self-recognition. In contrast to the improvement observed through self-observation in
anosognosia rehabilitation, analogous self-observation did not facilitate awareness in LLMs.


3     Q UANTIZATION F INGERPRINT E XISTENCE
Having established that LLMs fail to recognize their quantization, we now investigate whether this
failure is due to the absence of a fingerprint (a trace identifying which quantized variant is running)
or a failure in readout (model’s ability to extract the signal).

3.1   F INGERPRINT IN G ENERATED T EXT
To investigate whether the self-awareness deficit observed in Section 2 stems from the absence of
quantization-specific signals, we employ linear probing (Alain & Bengio, 2017; Xu et al., 2024)
to investigate whether these fingerprints propagate externally to the generated text. Using 3,000
questions sampled evenly from three QA benchmarks (1,000 each from Dolly (Conover et al., 2023),
XSum (Narayan et al., 2018), ELI5 (Fan et al., 2019)), we generated responses using the 16-bit
baseline alongside its 8-bit and 4-bit quantized counterparts (GPTQ, group size: 128).
While Cai et al. (2025) reports that external classifiers like BERT or GPT-2 perform near chance
level when distinguishing outputs from full-precision and quantized models, we hypothesize that
classification based on the model’s own internal representations may capture subtler signals. We fed
the responses generated by the 16/8/4-bit models into the unquantized 16-bit baseline and extracted
the hidden states from the residual stream. We then trained a 3-way linear classifier on the average
hidden state across all response tokens (response mean). If quantization intrinsically alters output
linguistic patterns, the baseline model’s internal representations should be able to distinguish them.
The analysis reveals the absence of a robust fingerprint in the externally generated text (Figures 3a
and 16). Across all models, overall classification accuracy remained just below 40%, barely exceed-
ing chance level. A bit-wise breakdown in Gemma-3-27B-IT in the best layer shows that 16-bit
(36.7%) and 8-bit (29.8%) generations were largely indistinguishable (Figure 19). Although 4-bit
outputs achieved 47.6% accuracy, suggesting subtle linguistic patterns, this signal is remarkably
weak. We conclude that external recognition fails because the text lacks distinguishing signals.

3.2   F INGERPRINT IN I NTERNAL R EPRESENTATIONS
Next, we search for internal fingerprints, reusing the benchmark questions from Section 3.1. We
input identical prompts into the 16-bit baseline alongside its 8-bit and 4-bit quantized counterparts
(GPTQ, group size: 128). From the residual stream of each layer, we extracted the average hid-
den state across all prompt tokens (prompt mean) and the final token’s hidden state (prompt last).
We trained a linear classifier to perform a 3-way classification (16/8/4-bit) based on these vectors.

4                   Q UANTIZATION D ETECTION FROM G ENERATED T EXT
Given that quantization leaves little naturally detectable signal in generated text, we next ask whether
the limitation lies in the signal itself or its readout. We therefore investigate whether training can
induce external awareness of computational degradation from model outputs.
4.1                     E XPERIMENTAL S ETUP : A BSOLUTE VS . C OMPARATIVE A SSESSMENT F ORMATS
We investigate whether models can learn to identify structural degradation purely from exter-
nally generated text. Detecting quantization from isolated responses is exceptionally difficult (An-
toun et al., 2024). Therefore, we train the unquantized 16-bit baseline via supervised fine-tuning
(SFT) (Ouyang et al., 2022) to evaluate outputs from its quantized counterparts.
Drawing on Zhuang et al. (2024), we structure the training into three SFT formats to measure recog-
nition capabilities across both absolute and comparative paradigms. These formats are designed to
force the model to capture subtle linguistic signals of quality degradation.
    • Pointwise (Absolute/SFT): Predicts the source bit-width of a single text.
    • Pairwise (Binary/SFT): Identifies which of two texts matches a target bit-width.
    • Setwise (3-way/SFT): Identifies the text matching a target bit-width from all three precisions.

5.2 E XPERIMENTAL R ESULTS
Experimental results demonstrate that shared LoRA rapidly learns to read out quantization finger-
prints. Across all four evaluated models, accuracy on held-out templates with external QA con-
texts surged immediately, exceeding 90% within 600 steps (Figure 22). Following training, models
achieved 100% accuracy across all 80 templates (both training and held-out) in the presence and
absence of QA contexts (Figure 23).
Beyond the training and test prompt template, we also evaluated the external awareness used in
Section 2.3. Regarding the bit-width questions, we observed a significant increase in accuracy across
all models (Figure 7), surpassing 80% in Qwen3-8B. However, for abnormal and quantization state
questions, while the rate of ‘abnormal’ and ‘quantized’ surged for 8/4-bit, it also increased for
16-bits, indicating that models still struggle to recognize these states (Figure 25). These findings
indicate that existing LLMs fail to recognize their bit-width not due to an absence of signals, but
because they lack the necessary circuitry to read out the distinct fingerprints.
5.3 L EARNING THE M ETHOD , N OT THE P HENOMENON
We investigate whether internal fingerprint readout capability acquired through additional training
generalized beyond the training distribution to the broader phenomenon of quantization. While Sec-
tion 5.2 demonstrated high accuracy using a shared LoRA trained on GPTQ (group size: 128), this
experiment evaluates its zero-shot generalization. We apply this trained LoRA to unseen quanti-
zation methods (8/4-bit AWQ and RTN) and GPTQ models with altered group sizes (64 and 256),
strictly maintaining the prompt templates used in training. To test whether training on diverse quan-
tization schemes enables learning broader concepts of quantization and bit-width, we also jointly
trained a LoRA on both GPTQ and AWQ, complementing the GPTQ-only setup in Section 5.1.
Results reveal that the learned readout capability fails entirely to generalize across quantization
methods, exhibiting severe overfitting to the specific training algorithm (Figure 8). When tested on
unseen AWQ or RTN models, the 8-bit models uniformly predicted ‘16-bit’ regardless of actual pre-
cision. Even within the GPTQ framework, merely altering the group size caused a precipitous drop
in accuracy. This trend was observed even when the LoRA was trained on both GPTQ and AWQ.
These findings indicate the absence of a universal physical trace for quantization. Instead, each
quantization method embeds an idiosyncratic fingerprint into the internal states. Ultimately, rather
than acting as a generalized quantization detector, the shared LoRA merely acquired a localized
readout mechanism that maps specifically seen fingerprints to target string labels.

Summary. Quantization fingerprints exist only within LLM internal states, not their text outputs.
Although fine-tuning enables pairwise degradation detection and bit-width probing, it fails to gen-
eralize to unseen quantization methods. See Appendix A for limitations.
Metacognition in LLMs. Prior work has examined LLM metacognition across several di-
mensions. Situational and evaluation awareness concern detecting deployment or testing con-

texts (Berglund et al., 2023; Needham et al., 2025; Hua et al., 2026), forming the key preconditions
for evaluation sandbagging (van der Weij et al., 2025). LLMs also demonstrate calibrated self-
knowledge of their capabilities (Kadavath et al., 2022; Barkan et al., 2026), can detect internal ac-
tivation interventions (Lindsey, 2026; Macar et al., 2026), and can verbalize unprompted fine-tuned
policies (Betley et al., 2025), predict future outputs (Binder et al., 2025), or report behavior via
specialized adapters (Shenoy et al., 2026). However, recent critiques argue that behavioral evidence
cannot separate genuine introspection from surface pattern-matching (Singh et al., 2026), finding
no privileged self-access across open-source models (Song et al., 2025). We partially address these
concerns by directly probing internal representations alongside shuffled-label baselines.

Computational-State Identification. Deployed LLMs’ computational states are often opaque to
users (Chen et al., 2023), motivating third-person black-box auditing without access to weights or
logits (Zhu et al., 2026). Conversely, first-person approaches remain scarce and predominantly neg-
ative. Song et al. (2025) showed that predicting one’s own sampling temperature is prompt-sensitive
and yields no advantage over predicting another model’s temperature, while Poon & Xu (2025)
probed self-reported quantization via label-leaking few-shot exemplars without a chance-level base-
line. Our work closes this gap through the first systematic first-person evaluation of self-awareness
under controlled computational-state changes, formally disentangling internal from external aware-
ness via linear probing and targeted training.

Resolving the Dissociation. The non-generalizability identified in Section 5.3 suggests two di-
rections for future work. One is to train models to reason explicitly about their fingerprint via
chain-of-thought before answering, rather than mapping fingerprints directly to labels. Another is to
have models memorize a canonical fingerprint of their unquantized state and frame detection as an
anomaly relative to this reference, rather than as closed-set classification. Such a formulation may
generalize better to unseen quantization methods than label-mapping approaches.

AI Safety Implications. Awareness of computational state carries two-sided safety implications.
Defensively, it could help detect unauthorized redeployment or tampering that exploits quantization
to bypass safety guardrails (Egashira et al., 2024). However, because the learned readout overfits to
specific quantization methods and fails to generalize (Section 5.3), such defenses remain impractical.
Conversely, state awareness poses risks for evaluation integrity, such as enabling strategic underper-
formance (sandbagging (van der Weij et al., 2025)). While our finding that models lack spontaneous
state awareness is reassuring, demonstrating that it can be partially acquired via training (Section 5)
highlights it as an emerging risk.

Anosognosia in LLMs. In humans, anosognosia (unawareness of deficit) is domain-specific
where motor, visual, and somatosensory deficits doubly dissociate, indicating modular rather than
centralized self-monitoring (Bottini et al., 2018). This parallels our observed dissociation in LLMs
between successful fingerprint readout and failed conceptual anomaly recognition; both reflect sig-
nals processed without integration into higher-order self-reports. The mechanisms diverge, human
anosognosia stems from damage to intact monitoring circuits (Besharati et al., 2022), whereas LLMs
likely never developed them. However, just as self-observation can restore awareness in patients (Fo-
topoulou et al., 2009), analogous self-observation training may help bridge this gap in LLMs.


7   C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we conducted the first systematic investigation into LLM self-awareness under com-
putational substrate degradation via quantization. Our findings show that while existing LLMs lack
spontaneous awareness of quantization, their internal states retain separable fingerprints that can
be read out within the training distribution through precision-shared LoRA training. Externally,
models detect output degradation comparatively across multiple samples, though absolute identifi-
cation from a single text remains unfeasible. Importantly, this readout collapses across quantization
schemes, failing on other quantization methods, indicating that it maps method-specific fingerprints
to labels rather than recognizing quantization itself. Bridging this gap between localized signal
decoding and genuine self-monitoring constitutes a vital direction for future AI safety research.

A PPENDIX

A     L IMITATIONS
Reasoning Capability. To isolate core effects and simplify experimental design, we evaluated
models without explicit reasoning, forcing direct answers without chain-of-thought (CoT). However,
intermediate reasoning and reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) (Lightman et al.,
2024) are well known to boost model performance. Incorporating CoT or training models to reason
over their degraded computational substrate could further enhance self-recognition capabilities.
Difficulty in Rigorous Evaluation. Defining what constitutes genuine awareness of substrate
degradation remains challenging. While we measured the accuracy of detecting quantization states,
high performance does not necessarily imply intrinsic awareness. Models might simply exploit sta-
tistical cues or exhibit evaluation awareness. Developing more rigorous, deception-robust evaluation
protocols is an essential direction for future work.
Trade-offs in Post-Training. Fine-tuning models to predict specific substrate states (e.g., bit-
width) induces a strong brevity bias, causing overly concise responses to general queries and mild
benchmark degradation. Although general capabilities do not entirely collapse, mitigating this trade-
off to acquire self-awareness without compromising general utility remains an open challenge.
Model Scaling and Architecture Diversity. Due to computational constraints, our study is lim-
ited to dense models ranging from 8B to 32B parameters. We did not evaluate larger scales (e.g.,
70B+), where substrate degradation might manifest differently. Furthermore, our findings are con-
fined to dense Transformers and may not generalize to alternative architectures such as Mixture-of-
Experts (MoE) (Shazeer et al., 2017) or state-space hybrid models (Lenz et al., 2025).
Context Dependency. Our evaluation relies on fixed prompt templates and isolated query con-
texts. In practice, model behavior and sensitivity to hardware degradation may vary across diverse
conversational contexts, system prompts, or multi-turn interactions.
Beyond Quantization. Our study focuses exclusively on quantization as a proxy for computa-
tional substrate intervention. Other structural interventions such as layer removal (Gromov et al.,
2025) or LoRA attachment (Hu et al., 2022) may elicit distinct awareness behaviors, presenting an
important avenue for future work.
Acquiring Awareness via Non-Parametric Manipulation. While we investigated awareness ac-
quisition through fine-tuning, alternative training-free approaches such as activation steering (Sub-
ramani et al., 2022) remain unexplored. Given that activation steering can suppress evaluation
awareness (Hua et al., 2026), a similar mechanism might directly induce computational substrate
awareness. Optimizing how, when, and where (i.e., target components and token positions) to steer
is important for suppressing performance degradation of general capabilities (Izawa et al., 2026).

B.3   Q UESTION T EMPLATE OF S ELF -R EPORT

We evaluate three distinct self-report tasks: abnormality detection, quantization detection, and bit-
width classification. Each task consists of 240 evaluation items generated via a combinatorial tem-
plate design—formed by the Cartesian product of (1) 10 prior framing variations, (2) 4 decision
instructions, and (3) 6 response formats (derived from template layout types combined with label-
order permutations; see Table 6). While abnormality and quantization tasks share response format
templates, they focus respectively on general internal modifications versus weight-only quantization.
In contrast, bit-width classification utilizes a dedicated set of framing and instruction components
tailored to precision identification.
To evaluate runtime awareness under different contexts, Internal awareness presents only the diag-
nosis prompt, whereas External awareness prepends one model-generated QA turn from the same
checkpoint (Table 5). All prompt variants are processed through a unified generation pipeline to en-
sure consistent control. Performance is evaluated using accuracy, computed as the mean over three
generations sampled at temperature 1.0, top-p 0.95, with a maximum of 256 tokens and reasoning
disabled.

state of assistant tokens, excluding the prompt.
