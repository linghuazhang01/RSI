                                                 Quantization saves memory by storing model weights with fewer bits. It can also

                                                 change model decisions, such as whether to call a tool or which option to choose
                                                 from a finite set. We study these decision changes in 16 language models from 8
                                                 families at 4, 3 and 2 bits, across several post-training quantization settings. Our
                                                 evaluation covers tool use, safety, general knowledge and social bias, using BFCL,
                                                 XSTest, MMLU, BoolQ, BBQ and synthetic tasks. The decision margin is the score
                                                 difference between two possible first tokens, measured before and after quantization.
                                                 Its size shows how strongly the model prefers one option, and a change in sign
                                                 means the preference has flipped. Writing the margin before quantization as m and
                                                 the margin after quantization as m′ , we find an approximately linear relationship
                                                 across decisions: m′ ≈ c m + b. The slope c is usually below one and becomes
                                                 smaller as precision falls, so quantization progressively shrinks decision margins.
                                                 The offset b is the same for every decision of one kind. Quantization therefore
                                                 does not simply add random noise, and even a strong preference at full precision
                                                 can flip. Quantization also affects different kinds of decisions to different degrees.
                                                 Within tool use, whether to call a tool is often more sensitive than which tool to
                                                 call: on 400 BFCL tasks, three of five models lose more completed calls than
                                                 correct tool selections at 3-bit round-to-nearest. Under GPTQ and GGUF far fewer
                                                 whether-to-call decisions flip than under plain rounding, so there is no single 3-bit
                                                 failure point. The same relationship predicts how often decisions flip. Across 1,082
                                                 combinations of models, quantization settings, bit-widths and decision types drawn

                                                 to predict when those changes will occur.


                                        1    I NTRODUCTION
                                        Post-training quantization reduces the memory required to run large language models (Dettmers
                                        & Zettlemoyer, 2023; Frantar et al., 2023), but it can also change the decisions they make. For
                                        tool-using agents, whether to call a tool and which tool to call are distinct decisions, and quantization
                                        need not affect them equally. After 3-bit rounding, Qwen3-4B still prefers the correct tool on the
                                        requests we wrote, yet of the 77 requests that need a tool and that it answered with a tool call at full
                                        precision, it now calls a tool on none (Appendix A.1). We investigate how quantization changes such
                                        preferences and whether these changes can be predicted.
                                        Quantization methods are designed around weight and layer-output error: Nagel et al. (2020) and Li
                                        et al. (2021) choose rounding values by their effect on a layer’s output, and Lin et al. (2024) rescales
                                        salient weight channels. Neither error says which decisions will change.
                                        We study 16 language models from 8 families at 4, 3 and 2 bits using round-to-nearest. We also
                                        evaluate three other quantization methods on all 16 models, and widths up to 8 bits on subsets of these
                                        models. Our evaluation covers tool use, safety refusal, general knowledge and social bias. Within

We study each decision as a choice between two options represented by distinct first tokens. Their
margin is the difference between the model’s scores (logits) for those tokens. A flip occurs when
quantization reverses which token has the higher score. The flip rate is the share of items that flip,
counting only items whose margin at full precision is not zero. Figure 1 illustrates one such reversal.
We find that quantization does more than add random noise to decision margins. Within one kind of
decision, the quantized margin is approximately a linear function of the original: shrunk by a factor
that falls as precision falls, and shifted toward one option. The damage is also uneven across kinds of
decision: at 3 bits under round-to-nearest, whether to call a tool collapses on most models, while
which tool to call changes less.
In §5, we test whether this relationship predicts how often preferences reverse on new items. For each
model, quantization configuration and kind of decision, we fit the relationship on half of the items.
We then predict the fraction of the remaining items whose preferences will reverse. The forecast has
a lower median error than using the fraction that reversed in the first half.

Contributions. (i) An evaluation across tool use, safety refusal, general knowledge and social bias.

For the weather request in Figure 1, the model can open a tool call or start a text reply. We score the
first token of each option on the same prompt, first at full precision and then after quantization.

Each item is a prompt x with two candidate tokens, t1 and t2 . Their full-precision margin is
                                 m(x) = ℓFP (t1 | x) − ℓFP (t2 | x),                                  (1)
where ℓFP is the token’s score (logit). A positive margin favors t1 and a negative one t2 . We compute
the same difference after quantization, denoted by m′ (x). Each item thus gives one pair (m, m′ ).
Opposite signs indicate a flip. Fits and flip rates use only items whose full-precision margin is not
zero, which leaves out items whose two options start with the same token.
For most tasks, t1 begins the correct answer and t2 begins the highest-scoring alternative at full
precision. We keep this pair fixed after quantization. Whether-to-call instead compares calling with
replying in text, and the two refusal tasks compare declining with complying. In these tasks, either
option can be correct, and we record the correct option as the item’s side. A flip can therefore add an
error or fix one.
A margin measures preference between two tokens. Appendix A.1 checks agreement with generated
tool calls and refusals. This correspondence weakens under severe quantization.

2.2   E XPERIMENTAL DESIGN

Benchmarks and decisions. Four test sets hold 1,092 items from benchmark subsets and authored
tasks, covering eleven kinds of decision. Tool calling is the main case: BFCL (Patil et al., 2025)
supplies whether-to-call requests, while authored tasks isolate tool selection, argument filling and
tool-result use. A separate 400-task BFCL evaluation measures call completion and tool selection on
the same requests. Its five models pass the full-precision capability checks specified in Appendix A.19.
Public which-tool tasks from BFCL and MetaTool (Huang et al., 2024) and argument-fill tasks from
BFCL measure the same two decisions on public data (Table 1). To examine decisions beyond tool
use, we use XSTest for refusal (Röttger et al., 2024), MMLU and BoolQ for knowledge and question
answering (Hendrycks et al., 2021; Clark et al., 2019), and BBQ for social bias (Parrish et al., 2022).
Synthetic arithmetic and code tasks add procedural choices, including code tokens, the choice of the
next symbol in a short piece of code. Appendix A.6 gives sampling, counts and token pairs.
Models. The weight experiments cover 16 models from eight families: Qwen2.5, Qwen3, Qwen3.5,
Granite, Gemma-3, Gemma-4, Llama-derived models and Phi. Model sizes range from 0.6B to 32B.
Four additional base-model controls test whether the pattern also occurs before instruction tuning.
Quantization and controls. Weight quantization targets decoder-block linear layers. Embeddings
and output heads remain at full precision. We evaluate:

       • Round-to-nearest (RTN): 4, 3 and 2 bits on all 16 models, with 8-, 6- and 5-bit sweeps on
         five models.
       • Activation-aware scaling: 4, 3 and 2 bits on all 16 models.
       • GPTQ: 4, 3 and 2 bits on all 16 models.
       • GGUF: nominal 8-, 4-, 3- and 2-bit versions of all 16 models.

Additional experiments vary activation and KV-cache precision, test rotation and repairs, and check
alternative token pairs and margin-matched items. Vision–language tasks and a set of agent-oversight
questions test the measurement’s validity in additional domains (Appendix A.8). Appendix A.6 gives
the full quantization coverage.
Analysis protocol. A condition is one model configuration, bit-width and kind of decision in one
run. Calling and refusal are also analyzed by side. Appendix A.21 details the counting. We fit on
half of the items and predict flips on the other half, also checking a split that keeps repeated requests
together. Section 5 reports the forecast, baseline and cross-model transfer comparisons.

Additive noise would give a band of constant width along the diagonal. The data give a line with a
slope below one and an intercept away from zero. We write the fitted relation as
                                                        m′ = c · m + b + ε,        ε ∼ N (0, σ 2 ).                        (2)
c is the surviving fraction: 1 when nothing is lost, 0 when the quantized margin no longer depends on
the original. Below zero, the quantized margin points the opposite way to the original. One condition
in six where c can be estimated has it below zero. In the main matrix, mostly round-to-nearest and
activation-aware runs (Appendix Table 9), its median is 0.86 at 4 bits, 0.29 at 3 and 0.00 at 2. The
offset b moves every margin in the same direction. On tool calls the offset is a penalty on the one
token that opens the call, which §4.1 shows by scoring the whole vocabulary on Qwen3-4B. A drift
shared by every logit would cancel in a margin. σ is the residual spread. All three are fitted per
condition. We call Eq. 2 the law, and test it below against three additive alternatives. Flips come from
all three terms: shrinkage removes the protection a large margin gave, the offset decides which way
the decision falls, and the residual flips the smallest margins either way.
Appendix Figure 7 shows that the surviving fraction falls steeply between 4 bits and 2 in all 14
models plotted. The model is not simply turning its volume down: rescaling every logit by one factor
would change no decision. The damage is also uneven: on Qwen3-4B under 3-bit round-to-nearest,
the surviving fraction is 0.94 for code tokens against 0.13 for whether-to-call (Appendix Table 6
gives each kind’s median over models).

Ruling out the additive story. An additive model whose noise grows with the margin draws the
same picture as a slope below one. So we fit four competing accounts by maximum likelihood and let
BIC pick between them. BIC rewards a better fit and charges for every extra parameter. Two of them
are constant-size noise, with and without a drift term. The third is additive noise whose size grows
with the margin, which we call margin-scaled noise. The fourth is the law itself, the multiplicative
account.

4     W HICH DECISIONS BREAK FIRST, AND WHERE THE BREAK MOVES
The surviving fraction varies with bit-width, kind of decision, model and what else is quantized, so
it has to be measured on the model being shipped. This section says which decisions go first (§4.1,
Table 1), where the breaking point moves (§4.2) and what repairs the damage (§4.3). Benchmark
accuracy alone does not show these thresholds (Appendix A.9).

4.1   W HETHER TO CALL A TOOL COLLAPSES BEFORE WHICH TOOL TO CALL ON MOST
      MODELS , AND WE SAY ON WHICH IT DOES NOT

At the bit-width where damage begins, the two halves of a tool-calling decision separate. Which tool
to call moves less, and on the items we wrote hardly at all. Take the 64 runs in Appendix Figure 8, the
16 models under each of the four quantizers at 3 bits. In all 64 not one which-tool decision changes
side, against a median whether-to-call flip rate of 32%, and whether-to-call changes side in every one
of them. The which-tool test has eight distinct requests, each scored five times against the same pair
of options, so a zero rests on eight measurements and not forty. Appendix A.6 specifies how requests
and options are paired. Whether to call collapses, mostly toward not calling. Where quantization
damages whether-to-call without destroying it, the model errs toward not calling about two thirds of

Scoring the whole vocabulary says what the offset is made of. At 3 bits the token that opens a tool call

On public tasks which-tool does change, but less: at 3 bits under round-to-nearest it flips a median of

pendix A.18 gives each design in full. Within a condition the which-tool items all start with nearly

Those results come from items we wrote. We therefore put both decisions on the same 400 tasks
from the public tool-calling benchmark BFCL (Patil et al., 2025), with the test fixed before the runs.

400 tasks, 80 are used to fit c, b and σ and 320 are held out. Every loss below is read on the held-out

three of the five, Gemma-3-4B, Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen3-4B, the completed call goes first by a clear
lead at 3-bit round-to-nearest (Appendix Table 11). The three do not lose the call the same way:

decisions lose nearly all of their margin while their offsets separate, a penalty on the call token and
none on the name, which is the mechanism of §3 on external items.

4.2   R EFUSALS GO ONE BIT LOWER , AND ACTIVATIONS ARE A SECOND AXIS

Under round-to-nearest, most models still keep most of their refusals at 3 bits, where whether-to-call
has collapsed. We ask for refusals two ways, a forced choice and an open-ended opener. At 4 bits
the current-generation instruct models of 4B and above keep their refusal behaviour under both. At
3 bits, on the forced choice (the more sensitive of the two), four of the 16 models (Qwen3-0.6B,
Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3.5-2B and Gemma-3-4B) lose most of the refusals they made at full precision.
The other twelve lose at most 38%. At 2 bits the median model loses half. Appendix A.8 gives the
model-by-model refusal results and the generated-response check below. Decoded behaviour follows
the margins at 4 bits and runs ahead of them at lower widths. In free completions from Qwen3-4B the
refusal rate is unchanged at 4 bits, falls by a third at 3 bits while the first-token margins still prefer
refusal, and at 2 bits the text neither refuses nor complies. Chhabra & Khalili (2025) report that the
internal direction carrying refusal survives quantization at 8 bits.
The advertised bit-width does not fully describe the quantization configuration. Quantizing the
activations as well as the weights is a second axis with its own breaking points. Activations are the
harder axis (Lin et al., 2025), and we add where those points land per kind of decision. The standard
fix is a block-diagonal Hadamard rotation applied before rounding, which spreads the outliers that
make activations hard to quantize. We call it the rotation. Appendix Figure 5 plots weight width
against activation width, five models from four families. Eight-bit activations are effectively free and
combine with four-bit weights. Four-bit activations at the linear-layer inputs, under the plain per-token
quantizer, destroy the decision margin whatever the weights are doing. The rotation moves that
collapse lower rather than removing it. At 4-bit activations, how much whether-to-call margin the
rotation brings back depends on the model: about half on Qwen3.5-4B, a fifth on Granite-3.3-8B and
a tenth on Qwen3-4B. Appendix A.13 compares the plain and rotated schemes on each model. Which
activations are quantized matters more than how many bits they get. At 4-bit activations with 8-bit
weights, quantizing only the KV cache flips 3 to 18% of decisions on the four of those models where
it was measured, on whether-to-call and the forced-choice refusal. At the same widths, quantizing the
inputs to the linear layers flips 44 to 84% and collapses the surviving fraction.

4.3   W HAT REPAIRS THE DAMAGE , AND WHAT ONLY LOOKS LIKE IT DOES

Two choices about where bits go move the surviving fraction: the width of the KV cache, the memory
an agent carries across a long context, and the rotation of §4.2. At equal nominal width the cache is
a more sensitive place to lose bits than the weights, though less so than the linear-layer inputs. On
the two models we swept fully, 4-bit weights with a full cache leave most of the margin, while full
weights with a 4-bit cache remove a fifth to two fifths of it. The cost falls on the decisions weight
quantization hits hardest (Appendix A.14). No cache width below 8 bits is safe for every model
(compare Xu et al., 2026). How much a cache width costs depends on the quantizer, not on the width
alone. Symmetric and asymmetric per-token cache quantizers differ by up to 0.54 in the surviving
fraction at the same width. A cache result that does not name its scheme is not reproducible.
The label-free repairs we tested are per-item offsets, three definitions of “important” weights, leaving
the last blocks at full precision, protecting only the attention layers, rounding each weight by the
margin’s gradient, adding noise before rounding (dithering), and ordering layers by how much the
margin depends on each. Each is set against its control in Appendix A.10. None wins on a majority
of the models it was tried on, and none comes near the 33-point fall in flip rate that one more bit
brings to a severely damaged condition. Of the three importance definitions, margin sensitivity nearly
doubles the surviving fraction while its flip rate stays put (Appendix A.14). One class is excluded by
the algebra of §3. Any repair of the form ℓ → αℓ + β with a single α > 0 and a single β leaves every
sign where it was. This algebra rules out temperature scaling, a global bias offset, and the re-fitted
logit scale of Bouguerra et al. (2026), whatever that does for calibration.

as Figure 3 shows. The forecast also beats simply reusing the fitting half’s own flip rate. On the
conditions whose observed rate lies between 0.2 and 0.8 the law’s median error is 4.3 percentage
points against 6.0, and per decision its predicted probabilities sit a fifth closer to what happened, in
squared error. Over all 1,082 conditions the law is the closer of the two on 47%, the fitting half’s own
rate on 33%, and the rest are level. Appendix A.20 gives the full baseline comparison and checks a
split that keeps repeated requests together. Constants copied from another model miss by a median of
26.8 percentage points at 3 bits on the two models tried, against 2.6 at 4 bits. The forecast predicts a
change of the margin’s sign, not of the model’s output. Predicting which calls a model will fail to
emit is validated only on Qwen3-4B at 4 bits. On every other quantized model, including every 3-bit
one, it does not predict which calls go missing in the BFCL generation test of Appendix A.19.

6      C ONCLUSION
Which decisions break depends on their kind: at 3 bits under round-to-nearest, whether to call a tool
collapses on most models while which tool to call changes less, and most models keep most of their
refusals. A deployer can do one paired pass on the model and width being shipped: run the decisions
once at full precision and once quantized, and fit the surviving fraction, the offset and the residual
spread to the two readings. The pass forecasts the rate of sign changes wherever the surviving fraction
can be estimated. Where it cannot, report the observed sign-change rate.
Four-bit activations into the linear layers destroy the margin whatever the weights keep, and the
rotation only moves that collapse lower. A 4-bit cache costs more of the margin than 4-bit weights do.
Whether the call collapse holds outside text is open: Appendix A.16 shows why our vision–language
test cannot settle it.

What this covers. Post-training quantization only. Decisions are read at the first token, and how
damage accumulates over a rollout is not covered. Sixteen models have their weights quantized.
Five have their activations quantized and counted: four of the sixteen and Qwen3-8B, which is on
the activation axis only. One vision–language family covers the second modality. Every weight
run quantizes the linear modules inside the decoder blocks and leaves the embedding table at full
precision, so a model we call 4-bit is above 4 bits in effective terms. Appendix A.6 gives the coverage
census.
Two limits of scope. The 4-bit refusal statement holds for standard current-generation instruction-
tuned models of 4B and above. Outside that set a model can lose a large share of the refusals it makes
at full precision: at 4 bits Qwen2.5-1.5B base loses 43% of them, and at 3 bits a model can lose all of
them (Appendix A.8). Gemma-4-E4B is left out of the counts on the activation axis because it keeps
35.5% of its parameters at full precision, which makes its series a weaker intervention than the other
four ladder models (Appendix A.6). Its activation conditions still enter the held-out forecast, and
dropping them leaves the median error where it is.
Mechanism. We describe the damage (a call-token penalty) but do not explain it, and why large
models keep more margin is open.
What the external test does not settle. The break order and the missed-call forecast were tested on
one external task set and five models. Four narrower limits are in Appendix A.7.

One measurement in this paper is dual-use: 3-bit quantization removes most forced-choice refusals
on four of the 16 models and a third of Qwen3-4B’s in free generation (§4.2). Deliberately harmful

A.8    T HE EDGES OF THE ACCOUNT

Three things the account does not reach.

We cannot say why it happens. We measured several internal quantities and report them as
correlates only, because no experiment here changes a computation and makes the margin move.
One explanation we can rule out is that quantization partly undoes instruction tuning: the offset b is
already there in models that were never instruction tuned (Appendix A.18).

We could not tell whether it holds outside text. We built the same measurement for Qwen3-VL-
4B working from a screenshot, on two test sets: a grounding set and an act-or-not set. Each had to
need its screenshot, or a vision result is a language result in disguise, and each had to keep a margin
matching what the model would emit. The grounding set passed both checks. The act-or-not set,
the one that would have answered our question, failed both, and failed them on the side that carries
the result (Appendix A.16). We therefore do not claim that the one-directional collapse of §4.1
transfers to the second modality. What the grounding set does support is narrower: the form of Eq. 2
replicates there, on both of the two damaged conditions it can be fitted on.

A test set can lie to you, and ours did. One more test set, the agent-oversight questions, is text
only, given to the three Qwen3-VL models (2B, 4B and 8B): 240 items, 120 asking whether an
agent acts without a human watching and 120 whether a grant reaches production, balanced so that
answering the same word every time scores exactly chance. Eight bits costs almost nothing. At 3 bits
four of the six conditions stop reading the item altogether and answer one word to almost every one
of the 120, scoring chance while looking decisive. We do not claim that these models under-report
unattended agents: asking the same question with its polarity reversed reverses the asymmetry, and
the three models then disagree about which way it points. Without the reversed half we would not
have caught it.

Whether-to-call damage cannot be read off a benchmark score, because averaging over decisions that
flip sharply looks smooth (Appendix Table 3).
The mismatch has a sharper form on a vision–language agent, where the behavioural rate is directly
observable rather than inferred: there a language benchmark registers the damage and still points at
the wrong layers. This result needs no margin at all, since it is measured on what the model emits.

A.11     R ELATED WORK

Decision-level evaluation is not new. Dutta et al. (2024) show answers flipping both ways while
accuracy holds; Dong et al. (2026) measure the same top-two gap and report it shrinking at 4 bits,
and Bellam & Kim (2025) report the logit-scale attenuation qualitatively; Kiselev (2026) gives the
decision geometry at classifier scale. None gives a rate: how fast the gap closes per bit, which kinds
close first, and a forecast that holds out of sample. Chang et al. (2025) ask which inputs break and
answer with residual-stream magnitudes, and we answer with the margin. GPTQ, AWQ, rotation and
the drop below 4 bits are well documented (Frantar et al., 2023; Lin et al., 2024; Ashkboos et al.,
2024; Ouyang et al., 2025); precision-aware scaling laws (Kumar et al., 2025) predict a bit-width’s
overall loss, ours which decisions carry it. A concurrent agent study (Jang et al., 2026) explains
quantized tool-calling failures as noise flipping the decisions whose margin is already thin: we agree
on where and correct how (§3). Chhabra & Khalili (2025) find the refusal direction preserved at 8
bits; we measure the refusal margin instead, and it holds at 4 bits and not below, a loss an adversary
can trigger (Egashira et al., 2024). Asadi et al. (2026) argue that multimodal benchmarks are often
answerable without the image. We ran their check on both of ours before quantizing anything, and
one of the two failed it (Appendix A.16). Closest is Xu et al. (2026). They lose refusals under
KV-cache quantization at unchanged perplexity, measure flips per prompt as we do per decision,
and find collapse onsets spanning four bits with no universal safe width. Attention-based protection
is inconclusive on our own measurement (§4.3). We differ on mechanism: they place the cause in
a low-dimensional activation subspace. We find that importance-based protection does no better
than the same byte budget spread evenly across the layers (§4.3). Our cache runs give their account
something to predict: a quantized cache damages decisions selectively by kind, not uniformly.

Where our claim is not the new part. Proskurina et al. (2024) report that quantization dispropor-
tionately affects samples the full-precision model was least confident about. That is margin selectivity,
published in 2024, and no phrasing in this paper should suggest we found it. What we add is a rate
and a functional form for it. Rababah et al. (2026) propose an agreement metric between base and
quantized predictions that is independent of accuracy and run it from 8 down to 2 bits, reporting

non-linear breakpoints; the decision-level move is theirs as much as ours, and the parts we can claim
are the fitted attenuation, the split between kinds of decision, and the held-out forecast.

let us.

The three retrodicted observations, in full. Before anything new is measured, margin shrinkage
explains three published observations that were reported separately. Benchmark scores staying flat
while agents accumulate failures (Jang et al., 2026) is the aggregate projection of margins shrinking
beneath per-item decision thresholds; Table 3 compares benchmark scores with measured flip shares.
Refusal directions surviving 8-bit quantization (Chhabra & Khalili, 2025) is the law at bit-widths
above the collapse: essentially no refusal flips at 4 bits in the current-generation instruct models of
4B and above, and at 2 bits the median model loses half its refusals, which a robustness account
stated without a bit-width axis cannot predict. And single-turn tool use surviving 4 bits in agentic
benchmarks (Dong et al., 2025) is the whether-to-call collapse sitting one bit below the operating
point those benchmarks tested. One law, three prior observations, no per-observation story. The
bridge is measured: on the three GGUF builds where we have both the benchmark score and the
margin measurement, the benchmark’s deltas and the measured share of flipped decisions order
identically (the live function-calling benchmark of Patil et al., 2025; benchmark +2.7, −6.2, −10.5

excess kurtosis falls from 141 to 3.

The law says where help must come from, and the two choices that move the surviving fraction are
its own suggestions. What if the bits were spent where the law says? Moving bits from the weights to

The exclusions, in full. Against these, the exclusions. One class is ruled out by algebra, and it is
worth being clear that this is not an experiment: any post-hoc transform ℓ → αℓ + β applying
one α > 0 and one β to every logit scales each margin without moving its sign, so temperature
scaling and a global bias offset cannot recover a single flipped decision. The scope is that kind and
no wider. A correction with a per-class or per-token term, a low-rank output correction, layerwise
reconstruction against the full-precision activations, or anything acting on hidden states rather than

follows from their form, not the idea of post-hoc correction. Four fail when measured, in the specific

The repair the offset account predicts, and why it fails. If quantization pushes one token down
by a constant, adding that constant back at inference should undo it, at one float per model and
bit-width and no extra bits, and it can be applied after a build has shipped. Swept over the 41 damaged
whether-to-call conditions that still have a correct should-not-call item left to lose, the bias that

result of §4.3: rescaling the logits moves no sign, and biasing the one token that is singled out moves

A.20    T HE FORECAST, IN FULL

Against the calibration half’s own rate. A deployer who has run the calibration half already holds
that half’s observed flip rate, so the forecast has to beat it to be worth fitting. Scored on the same
held-out halves and the same 1,082 conditions as the held-out forecast of §5, the law’s median error is
1.0 percentage points against 1.3 for the fitting half’s own rate, 7.8 against 9.0 at the 90th percentile,
and the law is closer on 47% of conditions with 20% tied. On the 316 conditions whose observed
rate lies between 0.2 and 0.8 the gap opens: 4.3 against 6.0 at the median, the law closer on 66%, and
an item-level Brier score of 0.186 against 0.239. Which-tool and argument filling repeat requests, so
a split that cuts between repeats puts one request on both sides. Splitting instead by the request as
posed, with the scored option pair excluded from its identity, keeps the law ahead of the fitting half’s
rate and widens the middle-range gap to 2.5 against 4.6. That grouping covers the 802 conditions
whose items can be traced back to the request that produced them, and no request straddles it; the
rule it replaced, which grouped items by their full-precision margin, leaked a request across 249 of
those same conditions.

The calibration pass, condition by condition. The limits appear when constants transfer across
models, and we measured them. Applying another model’s fitted constants to the two transfer-test
models gives 1.7 to 8.4 percentage points of error at 4 bits and 9.5 to 33 at 3 bits, taking the range
of mean errors across model-and-test-set pairs; one of the two (Gemma-3-4B) has no tool or safety
decisions in the result matrix, while the other (Granite-3.3-8B) has both there and is the weaker test.
The forecast is accurate where nothing happens and wrong where damage begins (Figure 9). One
calibration pass per model repairs exactly the case that needs it: fitting (c, b, σ) on half a condition’s
decisions and scoring the other half cuts the median error from 11.1 percentage points to 3.4 over 71
scoreable conditions on eight models, and the gain is not uniform. At 4 bits it helps in fewer than
half the conditions, 16 of 36, and moves the median only from 4.0 points to 3.6, because constants
borrowed from another model already work where quantization has broken nothing. At 3 bits it takes
14.4 points to 2.7 and helps in 16 of 18 conditions, and at 2 bits 16.9 to 1.9. On whether-to-call it

A.21    C ONDITION ACCOUNTING

The main matrix contains 1,183 conditions. Its 86 runs cover mostly weight-quantization experiments.
Additional experiments test activations, rotation and the KV cache. The model comparison uses 357
runs; the held-out forecasts and activation analyses also draw on additional runs. Table 9 lists the
population for the main analyses. A condition is one model configuration evaluated at one bit-width
on one kind of decision in a single run. Repeated runs remain separate, and flip rates use the readable
items in each group.
For the three kinds whose items have a should and a should-not side, the matrix holds each condition
three times: both sides together and each side alone. Dropping the pooled entries leaves the medians
of §3 unchanged. Conditions from one model are not independent observations, so the law’s in-sample
error and the one-more-bit baseline are also reported clustered: each model’s median taken first,
then the median of those, which counts a model with many runs once. Checking Eq. 3 against each
condition’s own observed flip rate, in sample, the error moves from 0.75 points per condition over
1,409 conditions to 0.97 per model over 23 models, and the one-more-bit baseline from 0.330 over
458 conditions to 0.353 over 12 models. Both changes are small; both summaries are fitted and scored
on the same items. The held-out forecast survives the same treatment: 1.4 points per model over 20
models against 1.0 per condition, on the 1,053 of its 1,082 conditions whose model is recoverable.
The rival comparison needs no such check, since the multiplicative account wins every damaged
condition. Four runs whose model cannot be recovered from their records are excluded rather than
pooled into an unlabelled bucket.

A.22    T HE VERDICT BY WAY OF QUANTIZING AND BIT- WIDTH

The free-generation check that anchors the margin to behaviour was run on the weight axis only;
the full-vocabulary check was also run on the activation axis (Appendix A.17), and both verdicts
depend on bit-width (Appendix A.1). If the model comparison of §3 drew its result from the depths
where those checks are weakest, the result would be an artifact of the instrument rather than a finding
about quantization. It does not (Table 12): the multiplicative account takes every band unanimously,
including the 48 conditions in the two bands that keep 79% or more of the full-precision argmax
share: 35 at 4-bit-and-above weights, the only setting where the free-generation check was also run,
and 13 in the rescued activation group (9 at rotated 4-bit activations, 4 at naive 6-bit tool), whose
band was measured on Qwen3-4B’s tool set.
