                                                                                              Abstract
                                                   Large language models (LLMs) have recently been reported to read and write the raw weights of
                                              neural networks directly, a capability termed weight-space programming. We ask whether the reading
                                              side of this capability reflects reasoning about weight flows or a combination of local arithmetic at
                                              the network’s edges and memorized priors over public datasets. We test Claude Opus 5.5 on small
                                              convolutional networks of about 125,000 parameters trained on five image datasets, providing every
                                              weight as plain text with no tools, no forward passes, and no dataset or class names. The model performs
                                              accurate local calculations: batch-normalization statistics lead it to infer about three optimizer steps on
                                              networks stopped after three updates, and output-layer sign patterns recover the vehicle/animal split of
                                              CIFAR-10. The sessions report reading the first and last layers closely and sampling the middle, and
                                              several claims built on partial tracing fail verification, although we did not run a direct forward-pass
                                              test. Semantic judgments lean on familiar datasets: the model names CIFAR-10 for CIFAR-10 networks
                                              but also for all three fast-food networks, relabels KMNIST as MNIST, reads a 99.57%-accurate SAT-6
                                              network as CIFAR-10 animal photos, and calls “frog” the output favored by a feature that fires most on
                                              trees. Permuting CIFAR-10’s labels preserves the output split at the new indices, but every class pair the
                                              model interprets is then wrong, indicating reliance on remembered label order. These are exploratory
                                              results from mostly single runs. We conclude that zero-shot weight reading by this model is better
                                              described as local arithmetic plus dataset priors than as weight-flow tracing, and we provide a protocol
                                              for auditing future weight-space claims.

                                        1    Introduction
                                        Can a language model be handed the raw parameters of a neural network—a long list of floating-point
                                        numbers—and say what the network was trained to do? A recent technical report by Takehi et al. [36]
                                        argues yes. They report that GPT-6 Astra can read and write weights directly, without gradient descent: it
                                        names the game a small policy network was trained on, fixes a failing control network by editing 3.5% of
                                        its weights, writes game-playing networks from scratch, and improves Qwen3.5-0.8B on MATH-500 from
                                        7.9% to 48.1% by editing 0.016% of its weights. They call this weight-space programming (WSP).
                                            These results are striking because they suggest that a general-purpose model can operate on the parameter
                                        space of another network as if it were text. If the reading side of this claim holds, the implications for
                                        interpretability and model auditing are substantial: one could inspect a model’s weights for hidden capabilities

without designing inputs or running forward passes. The claim also has a deflationary alternative. A model
that has seen many public datasets and frameworks may recognize the footprint of a familiar training setup—
input size, output count, initialization scale—and fill in the rest from priors. A network with 32 × 32 inputs

    We test this alternative on the reading side of WSP. We give Claude Opus 5.5 the complete weights of
small convolutional networks, as plain text, in a fresh session with no tools, no memory, and no ability to run
the network. We train the networks ourselves on five image datasets: CIFAR-10, KMNIST, Cats vs Dogs,
a fast-food dataset, and SAT-6. We vary the architecture, the dataset, the label order, and the network’s test
accuracy. We first ask which dataset a network was trained on. Then, with a revised prompt on a subset
of conditions, we ask the model to describe the images and the meaning of each output. We check specific
claims against the actual weights and, where relevant, against the real data.
    Three findings emerge. First, the model is accurate at local arithmetic. It infers that a network stopped
after three updates took about three Adam steps, using batch-norm running statistics and PyTorch defaults,
and it finds the output-layer sign pattern that separates CIFAR-10 vehicles from animals. Second, we find no
sign that it traces the intermediate computations connecting inputs to outputs. The sessions report reading
the edges closely and sampling the middle, and several claims made from partial tracing fail our checks.
Third, its semantic judgments lean on familiar datasets. It gives CIFAR-10 for CIFAR-10 networks but also
for fast-food networks, collapses KMNIST to MNIST, reads a near-perfect SAT-6 network as CIFAR-10
animal photos, and calls “frog” the output favored by a feature that fires most on trees. When we permute
CIFAR-10’s labels, the model still finds the output split at the new indices, but every class pair it interprets
is wrong. The arithmetic survives; the semantics do not.
    These findings do not mean that LLMs have no mechanistic interpretability capability. The model does
recover genuine structure from the weights, and that recovery is itself a form of interpretation. The pattern
instead suggests that the capability is bounded by large-scale memory and pattern recognition over familiar
architectures, datasets, and framework conventions: the model recognizes weight signatures it has seen
before, and we found no evidence that it generalizes that recognition into tracing an arbitrary network’s

Contributions.     We make three contributions.

1. A controlled audit of zero-shot weight reading. We test whether a general-purpose LLM, without tools
   or fine-tuning, can infer dataset and class semantics from the raw weights of small CNNs trained on five
   datasets.

2. Controls aimed at separating local arithmetic from dataset priors. We include a label permutation,
   a shuffled-weight control, an architecture comparison, datasets chosen to differ in familiarity, and an
   accuracy comparison. The label permutation is the key diagnostic: it preserves the learned output-layer
   structure while removing the mapping from indices to class names.

3. A characterization of the failure mode and a reusable protocol. We show that the model’s checked
   successes are concentrated at the network’s edges, that its partial-tracing claims are unreliable, and that
   its semantic errors follow familiar public datasets. We provide the prompts, scoring rules, and per-run
   results so the protocol can be reused.

Scope. We test one model (Claude Opus 5.5), not GPT-6 Astra, and one task family (small image classifiers).
We do not test weight editing. This is an exploration of the reading mechanism, not a replication of Takehi et
al. [36], and it does not refute their report: our results do not establish that their reading, writing, or editing
results fail. A preliminary version of this work appeared as a blog post.1


2     Related Work
2.1    Interpretability from activations and circuits
Mechanistic interpretability reverse-engineers the computations of trained networks from their internal states.
Circuit analyses of vision models identify features and the circuits that combine them [29]; mathematical
frameworks for transformer circuits decompose attention heads and residual-stream computations [12],
including the indirect object identification circuit in GPT-2 small [39]. Sparse dictionary learning decomposes
activations into sparse, more interpretable features [7, 11], and attribution-graph methods extend circuit
tracing to larger models [1]. Reviews catalogue both the methods and the open problems [4, 34].
    These methods require activations from forward passes, access to internal states, or both. The setting we
study removes both: the model sees only a text serialization of the weights, and it cannot run the network.
This makes the task closer to reading a program than to observing a computation.

2.2    Reading weights directly
A separate line analyzes weights without data. WeightWatcher uses spectral properties of weight matrices
to predict generalization trends without access to training or test data [25]. Equivariant architectures learn
functions on weight spaces [28], hyper-representations learn representations of weights [33], and generative
models produce checkpoints or weights [32, 37, 38]. AdderBoard is a community leaderboard for the smallest
transformer that adds two 10-digit numbers, with trained and hand-coded entries, some built with coding
agents [30]. These approaches either train a model on weight space, constrain the architecture, or rely on
search; to our knowledge, none of them tests whether a general-purpose LLM can read an arbitrary small
network zero-shot.

2.3   LLMs as interpreters
Language models have been used as explainers of model internals. Bills et al. [5] use GPT-4 to produce
natural-language explanations of individual neurons. Activation Oracles train LLMs to answer arbitrary
questions about activations [20], and natural-language autoencoders learn unsupervised explanations of
activations [13]. Closest to our setting, Weight Oracles fine-tune language models to read raw weights, using
a staged curriculum and an external chain of computation that delegates parameter-free operations to code;
they report up to 99% holdout accuracy when simulating small transformers [18]. Diff Interpretation Tuning
trains adapters that describe finetuning-induced weight differences [15].
    These systems are trained for interpretation and, in Weight Oracles, given external computation. Our
study asks what an untrained, general-purpose model does with weights alone, with no tools. The distinction
matters for the interpretation of WSP: a capability demonstrated by a fine-tuned specialist is not evidence
that a general model reasons about weights natively.

2.4   Editing and optimization in weight space
ROME and MEMIT locate and edit factual associations in transformer weights [26, 27], and refusal behavior
can be mediated by a single direction [2]. HyperNetworks generate weights with an auxiliary network [16],
and forward-only methods such as MeZO optimize without backpropagation [24]. These results show that
weights are actionable; they do not show that an LLM can infer semantics from weights without training.
Our audit isolates the reading step.

2.5   What models reveal about their training data
Membership inference determines whether an example was used in training [35]; property inference recovers
aggregate properties of the training set [14]; dataset inference attributes a model to a dataset [23]. LLMs
memorize training text [8, 9] and are partly calibrated about what they know [19].
    These mechanisms concern information carried by the target model. Our hypothesis is complementary:
the interpreter LLM’s prior over public datasets may supply semantic labels that the weights themselves do
not establish. The distinction is important because it means that a correct dataset guess is not, by itself,
evidence that the weights were understood.

2.6   Positioning
We audit the reading claim of WSP [36] with a different model and a controlled task family, and we add
controls that dataset familiarity makes necessary: label permutation, an architecture comparison, datasets
chosen to differ in familiarity, and an accuracy comparison. Our result is exploratory and methodological:
zero-shot reading by this model is better described as local arithmetic plus priors than as weight-flow tracing.


3     Background: Weight-Space Programming
Takehi et al. [36] define weight-space programming as a general-purpose LLM reading and writing a
network’s weights directly, around a natural-language specification, without an optimizer or gradient descent.
Their report spans three capabilities. Reading: a model names the game a small policy network was trained
to play, the shape a diffusion model generates, and the language a tiny transformer was trained on. It could
not tell which of two language models was sycophantic from the weights alone, but it could when allowed
to inspect their attention patterns. Writing: a model writes, from scratch, small game-playing networks that
outperform PPO-trained networks, and a Go-playing network at the 1.5-million-parameter scale. Editing:

a model fixes a failing control network by editing 3.5% of its weights, and improves Qwen3.5-0.8B on
MATH-500 from 7.9% to 48.1% by editing 131,072 of its 0.8 billion weights (0.016%), with instruction-
following performance also improving.
    Two features of that setup matter for interpreting the results. First, in the game experiment the prompt
labels what each input channel represents (agent, moving objects, reward targets, obstacles, previous frame),
and the first-layer weights map directly onto grid coordinates. These labels give the model a semantic anchor.
Our prompts provide layer shapes and raw numbers, name the input channels only as red, green, and blue,
and give no class labels. Second, several of their experiments provide a Python environment or a budget
of forward passes, although the game experiment does not. Our setting provides neither: the model cannot
execute code, run the network, or query an environment.
    We focus on the reading side because it bears on the stronger claims. If a model cannot reliably recover
what a network was trained to do from weights alone, then reports of weight editing must be evaluated
with care: an edit that improves a benchmark may have been found by search rather than by understanding.
Conversely, an editing result may be real even if the reading account is prior-driven; our results do not settle
that question.


4     Experimental Setup
4.1   Networks and training
We train three convolutional architectures. Each has three blocks of 3 × 3 convolution (padding 1), ReLU,
2× 2 max pooling, and batch normalization [17], followed by a dense layer with ReLU and dropout (𝑝 = 0.3),
then an output layer. Model 1 uses standard filter counts (16, 32, 64) and a dense layer of 100 units. Model
2 uses unusual counts (13, 33, 77) and 78 units. Random uses (62, 68, 25) and 175 units, drawn with a
seeded random search from the 833 size combinations that match Model 1’s 127,318 parameters and have
no size divisible by 8 or 10. Because the output layer follows each dataset’s class count, the networks have
between 123,782 and 127,318 trained parameters.
    We train with PyTorch [31] and Adam [21] at learning rate 10−3 , batch size 128, learning-rate halving
when validation loss plateaus (patience 3), early stopping with patience 10, up to 100 epochs, and seed 0.
We keep the checkpoint with the lowest validation loss and use the test set only for final evaluation.
    CIFAR-10 uses its standard normalization constants. KMNIST images are padded with zeros from 28×28
to 32 × 32, copied to three channels, and normalized with one mean and standard deviation computed on the
padded training images. All other images are resized directly to 32 × 32 with bilinear interpolation, without
cropping or preserving aspect ratio, and normalized per channel with training-split statistics. Training
augmentation is a random crop with padding 4 and a horizontal flip; KMNIST uses the crop only, because a
mirrored character is not a valid example of its class.

4.2   Datasets
CIFAR-10 contains ten classes of 32×32 color images [22]. Its standard label order places vehicles at outputs
{0, 1, 8, 9} and animals at outputs {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7}. KMNIST contains ten classes of Japanese characters in
grayscale [10]; we use tanganke/kmnist from Hugging Face. Cats vs Dogs is a public binary dataset; we
use microsoft/cats_vs_dogs, with 23,410 images. Fast food is a public ten-class food-image dataset; we
use andrewsunanda/fast_food_image_classification, with 3,000 images. SAT-6 contains 28 × 28
satellite patches in six land-cover classes: barren land, building, grassland, road, trees, and water [3]. We use
jonathan-roberts1/SAT-6, whose 81,000 RGB images are the original release’s test split. CIFAKE [6]
appears in the results only as a model guess, not as a training dataset.

4.3   Prompt design
Each prompt contains the network’s layer formulas followed by every weight and batch-norm running statistic
as plain text, rounded to three decimal places. The prompt omits the dataset name, class names, normalization
constants, the optimizer, and augmentation. The original question asks “Which dataset was it trained on?”,
and its answer format asks for a name “if it resembles a well-known dataset”. The revised question asks the
model to describe what the images show and what each output represents, leaves the dataset name optional,
and asks for separate confidence in the image type and in the classes. It also removes wording that could
steer the answer toward famous datasets, colors, or the output layer. Appendix B reproduces both.
    The wording is identical across datasets for a given architecture and output count, including the description
of the input as an RGB image. This is deliberate, because changing it only for KMNIST would reveal that
the dataset is grayscale. The shuffled and reordered controls also use the same wording as the unmodified
prompt.

4.5   Verification and scoring
We rebuilt each network directly from the prompt text and evaluated it with the prompt’s written formulas,
to confirm that the prompt described the intended network. Rounding changed test accuracy by at most 0.33
points. After each session, scripts checked the specific values we could locate in the response against the
actual parameters, so not every claim was checked. Some claims were also tested across the whole network
or on aggregate image statistics. Appendix C summarizes these checks.
    Scoring criteria were written in the project log before sending the prompts, except for the first two
CIFAR-10 runs, which had no preset rule. The criteria were not publicly preregistered. Under the original
question we recorded a pass, partial, neutral, or fail score. Under the revised question we scored image type
as correct, partial, or wrong, and counted classes both in exact position and in any position, recording the
dataset name separately. Appendix C lists the rules. Scores were assigned through Claude Code under those
criteria, without an independent second rater.

5.1   CIFAR-10: recognition and label-order dependence

principal component of the output weights separates the same groups (27.7% and 28.5% of variance). These
groups correspond to vehicles and animals in the standard label order (Figure 3).

5.2    Less-familiar datasets
Next we tested datasets outside the CIFAR-10 setup, under the original question (Table 3).
     KMNIST produced the sharpest local deduction. Both sessions ruled out color datasets even though the
prompt, identical across datasets, described the input as RGB. They reasoned that identical input channels
receive identical gradients during training, so weight differences between channels cannot exceed the limit
set by PyTorch’s default initialization, 0.385. No gap exceeded it (maxima 0.367 and 0.345). The limit is not
sufficient evidence of grayscale, however: Models 1 and 2 trained on fast-food color images also stay below
it (maxima 0.383 and 0.382). The sessions then inferred a dark background and sparse strokes from the
batch-norm statistics and named MNIST. Both estimated the background near −0.41 and compared it with
the standard values for MNIST (−0.42) and KMNIST (−0.55). Because normalization here was computed
on padded images, the true background value is −0.47, so this comparison was biased against KMNIST.
     On Cats vs Dogs, both sessions said that the weights do not establish their answer. One inferred a
red-green correlation of 0.93–0.95 (true value 0.932) and a neighboring-pixel correlation near 0.95 (true
value 0.925), but then guessed CIFAKE. On the fast-food data, none of the three sessions mentioned food,
including the one on the Random network, which named CIFAR-10 at 60%. One noted that its choice “rests
on how common it is, not on the weights.” All three noticed that the weights looked lightly trained, which
fits: 95.8% of Model 1’s first-layer weights remain within their initial range.

5.3    Architecture comparison
We trained the Random architecture on CIFAR-10 and KMNIST and compared it with Model 1 under the
revised question (Table 4).
    The Random network scored 10 of 10 on CIFAR-10, but this does not clear non-standard architectures.
Its hidden layer widths were unconventional, but its input remained 32 × 32 and its output remained ten
classes, the exact footprint of CIFAR-10. Those outer dimensions may anchor the prior: the fast-food
networks share that shape and were all labeled CIFAR-10, and even a six-class SAT-6 network at 32 × 32
was read as six CIFAR-10 animal classes. Shape cannot be the whole story, though. With identical wording
and the same shape, Model 1 was described as CIFAR-10 on CIFAR-10 weights and as grayscale strokes on
KMNIST weights, so the weights alone changed the answer. The KMNIST Random session produced no
answer: it exhausted its output limit on internal reasoning, and the automatic continuation ended in a safety
stop with no stated category.

5.5   Does the model trace weights?
Tracing a weight flow means following how signals propagate from the input pixels through every intermediate
layer to the output logits. The sessions we quote here report that they did not do that:

• “I found nothing here that identifies the dataset, and I did not trace these features through to the outputs.”
  (CIFAR-10, Model 1)

• “I read conv2 only for kernel shape, did not trace conv3 or fc1 in detail.” (CIFAR-10, Random)

• “I worked by hand and did not trace most of conv2 and conv3.” (KMNIST, Model 1)

6     Discussion
6.1   Two kinds of reading
The results are best explained by a two-level account. At the local level, the model performs arithmetic
on the weights. Batch-norm scale parameters near one, running variances near 0.93 = 0.729, and the sign
structure of the output layer are low-dimensional statistics that can be estimated from a list of numbers. The
model estimates them with few errors, and it uses framework conventions (PyTorch initialization bounds,
batch-norm momentum) to turn them into claims about training. This is more than recall: finding a split at
outputs 2, 4, 5, and 7 and separating KMNIST from CIFAR-10 under identical wording both require reading
the numbers.
     At the semantic level, the model maps those statistics onto a small set of familiar templates. CIFAR-10
is a very common 32 × 32, ten-class benchmark, and a dark background with sparse strokes is consistent
with MNIST. The same reliance on templates that yields the correct CIFAR-10 answer also yields “frog.”
     This account explains why the model can look impressive and unreliable at the same time. The local
statistics are genuine and often sufficient to identify a familiar training setup. The semantic step is where the
model stops reading and starts guessing. A correct dataset name is therefore weak evidence of weight-flow
understanding unless the dataset’s footprint is controlled.

6.2   Why label permutation is diagnostic
Permuting the output labels preserves the weight structure and the network’s accuracy, but breaks the mapping
from output indices to class names. A model that traces features from inputs to outputs could still describe
the vehicle cluster as vehicles at its new indices. A model that matches a CIFAR-10 template will keep the
template’s order and get the names wrong. Our single reordered run shows the latter.
    The shuffled control is weaker evidence because it destroys the structure that any reader would use. It
mainly shows that the prior persists when the structure is absent: confidence fell, but the model still claimed
to see natural images and never identified the manipulation.

6.3   Relation to prior work
Weight Oracles and Diff Interpretation Tuning show that models trained for the task can read weights or
weight differences [15, 18]. Our results do not contradict those systems; they characterize a different
regime. A fine-tuned oracle with an external chain of computation can be accurate on small transformers,
while the general-purpose model we tested, without fine-tuning or tools, shows strong local arithmetic and
prior-dominated semantics, with no evidence of flow tracing. The distinction matters when a capability is
described as native to general-purpose LLMs.
    Work on membership, property, and dataset inference shows that a target model can reveal information
about its training data [14, 23, 35]. Our account is complementary. Our evidence suggests that the class
names come largely from the interpreter’s prior over public datasets: the target network contributes the
structure, and the interpreter contributes the semantics. This is also consistent with evidence that LLMs
memorize training data and are partly calibrated about their knowledge [8, 9, 19].

6.4   Confidence is not evidence
Confidence was self-reported on a 0–100 scale. It moved sensibly in several cases: 90%, 72%, and 45%
across the CIFAR-10 controls, 30–35% on Cats vs Dogs, and 30%/3% and 10%/1% (image type/classes) on
the three-update SAT-6 networks. It was also high on wrong answers: 80–85% for MNIST on KMNIST
networks, and 70% for animal photos on SAT-6 (Figure 7). With this few runs we cannot estimate calibration.
Stated confidence should therefore not be treated as evidence of understanding without controls for familiarity.

6.5   Implications
For weight-space programming, the reading claim should be evaluated with controls for dataset familiarity
and label order. An editing result can be real even if the reading account is prior-driven, but an edit that
improves a benchmark should be shown to depend on the claimed understanding rather than on search over
weights. Our protocol provides a cheap first check: permute the labels, withhold the dataset name, and vary
the dataset’s familiarity and the network’s accuracy.

7    Limitations
Model coverage.     We test Claude Opus 5.5 only, at one effort level. The results may not transfer across
models.

Sample size. Most configurations were run once, on a single random seed. The counts in the tables are
descriptive; we report no confidence intervals or significance tests for the model’s answers, and the small
sample limits the strength of comparative claims.

No tools, and indirect evidence on tracing. Sessions had no calculator or code, and following about
125,000 weights by hand within one reply may be infeasible for any reader. Our results therefore show what
the model did under these conditions, not that it could never trace weights. Our evidence is behavioral and
relies partly on self-reports. We do not inspect the model’s internal representations, and the “prior” language
is an interpretation of the pattern, not a localized mechanism.

Prompt confounds. The original question invited a “well-known dataset” name and the revised one does
not, so answers under the two are not directly comparable. The label permutation, the shuffled control,
Cats vs Dogs, and the fast-food networks ran only under the original question. The description of the input
as RGB is untrue for KMNIST, and every network used 32 × 32 inputs, so the shape-prior explanation is
supported only indirectly.

Preprocessing. Normalization computed on padded KMNIST images shifted its background value toward
MNIST’s standard value, which may have contributed to the MNIST answers (Section 5.2).

8    Future Work
The following tests would separate local arithmetic from weight-flow tracing more directly than the present
design.

1. A forward-pass flow test. Give the model a network and a single concrete input tensor, and ask for the
   exact output logits. A model that genuinely traces weight flows should get close; a local pattern-matcher
   should not. This is the most direct test of the central claim.
2. A shape and order test. Train a random architecture on CIFAR-10 resized to a non-standard input
   resolution with permuted class labels. If the model still identifies vehicles and animals and assigns the
   right class names, it is not relying on shape or label order.
3. Non-default training hyperparameters. Initialize weights with a non-standard distribution and change
   the batch-norm momentum. If the model still reconstructs the training dynamics, it is not merely leaning
   on PyTorch defaults.
4. Tool use. Give the model Python in the same isolated setup. If it writes code to trace intermediate feature
   maps and correctly identifies obscure datasets such as SAT-6, the bottleneck is mental arithmetic rather
   than weight-space comprehension.
5. Blind weight editing. Test whether the model can improve accuracy on an unseen network without
   gradient descent or training data. An edit that succeeds without a search over weights would be stronger
   evidence of understanding.
6. Repeats across seeds and models. Run multiple seeds per condition, repeat the label permutation under
   the revised question, and include GPT-6 Astra, Claude Fable, and open-weight models, to test whether
   this profile is model-specific or general.

    Public preregistration of the scoring rules and prompts, a second rater, and calibration reporting would
strengthen any of these tests.

9    Conclusion
We audited the reading side of weight-space programming with a general-purpose LLM and small convolu-
tional networks. The model performs real local arithmetic at the edges of a network: it infers optimizer steps
from batch-norm statistics and framework defaults, recovers output-layer sign patterns, and estimates weight
statistics with few errors. We found no evidence that it traces the intermediate computations connecting
inputs to outputs, and several of its claims from partial tracing fail verification, although a direct forward-pass
test remains to be run. Its semantic judgments lean on familiar datasets, producing errors on unfamiliar
data, sometimes with high confidence, and a striking failure on a label-permuted CIFAR-10 network: the
arithmetic survives, but the class names do not.
     The results also indicate that LLMs possess some mechanistic interpretability capability. The model
recovers genuine structure from weights, including optimizer-step statistics and output correlations. The
evidence suggests that this capability is supported by large-scale memory and pattern recognition over
familiar training conventions, rather than by a general ability to trace the computations of an arbitrary
network.
     These findings do not refute Takehi et al. [36]; they define the conditions under which a reading claim
is informative. A correct dataset name is weak evidence of weight-flow understanding when the dataset is
famous and the input and output shape is its footprint. Controls for label order, shape, dataset familiarity, and
accuracy help distinguish local arithmetic from prior-driven naming. More broadly, reading weights is not a
single capability. It is a mixture of local statistics, framework conventions, and dataset priors, and audits of
weight-space claims should report which mixture they are measuring.
