                                        Abstract
                                        Weight-Space Programming (WSP) enables reasoning agents to directly inspect and modify neural network
                                        parameters, offering an alternative to gradient-based model adaptation. However, its capabilities, cross-task
                                        generalization, and practical limitations remain insufficiently understood. We systematically investigate WSP
                                        through 16 editing trajectories across seven benchmarks and two model scales (Qwen3.5-0.8B and Qwen3.5-
                                        9B). Our findings reveal three key insights. First, WSP can achieve substantial performance gains through
                                        highly localized interventions. On full held-out evaluations, accuracy improves by up to 29.45, 4.42, 36.72,
                                        and 17.11 percentage points on MATH-500, GPQA Diamond, IFEval, and SciBench, respectively. Notably,
                                        modifying only 1,023 parameters yields a 29.45-point improvement. Second, WSP exhibits promising but
                                        uneven cross-task generalization. A Math-edited checkpoint improves GPQA Diamond, IFEval, and SciBench
                                        by 3.03, 12.18, and 20.10 points, respectively, without further editing, while leaving SciCode performance
                                        unchanged. Third, effective editing remains challenging and inconsistent. Outcomes vary substantially across
                                        trajectories, and six of the 16 trajectories retain the original weights when candidate improvements cannot be
                                        reliably validated. Together, these results highlight WSP’s potential for highly localized model adaptation
                                        while revealing important challenges in intervention discovery, reliability, and cross-task transfer.

1    Introduction
Adapting pretrained neural networks typically relies on optimizing model parameters using task-specific data,
as exemplified by parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods such as LoRA [1]. Prior work has also demonstrated
that model behavior can be altered through direct weight interventions, including knowledge editing [2, 3] and
task-vector arithmetic [4]. Recently, Weight-Space Programming (WSP) has introduced a different approach to
model adaptation, enabling reasoning agents to inspect, interpret, and directly modify neural network parameters [5].
Rather than relying on gradient-based optimization or predefined editing procedures, WSP allows agents to diagnose
model behavior, identify candidate computational mechanisms, and construct targeted weight modifications. This
offers a promising direction for adapting pretrained models without conventional fine-tuning, potentially reducing
the dependence on large task-specific training datasets while enabling more deliberate interventions in the weight
space.
Early results demonstrate the potential of this approach. In the original WSP study, GPT-6 Astra improves the
MATH-500 accuracy of Qwen3.5-0.8B from 7.9% to 48.1% by modifying only approximately 0.016% of its
parameters, primarily addressing reasoning loops that prevent the model from completing its responses [5]. The
resulting improvements also extend to instruction following, suggesting that localized interventions can affect
behavior beyond their original target. However, the same study reports limited success when attempting to improve
a model without such identifiable failure modes. These observations raise a fundamental question:To what extent
can reasoning-driven weight interventions serve as a general approach to model adaptation, rather than a means
of repairing specific behavioral failures? Despite these promising initial results, the effectiveness of WSP across
diverse tasks, model scales, and independently executed editing processes remains insufficiently characterized.
To address this question, we investigate WSP along three complementary dimensions. Capabilities: How effectively
can WSP improve different model abilities, and how do the structure and scale of weight interventions relate
to their outcomes? Generalization: Do improvements obtained through task-specific editing transfer to other
capabilities without additional modifications, or do their effects remain confined to the target task? Limitations:
How reliably can agents discover, validate, and select effective interventions, and how do editing outcomes
vary across trajectories and model scales? These questions are particularly important because successful local
modifications do not necessarily imply broader behavioral improvements, as also observed in prior studies of model
editing and generalization [6].
In this work, we conduct a systematic empirical investigation of WSP through 16 editing trajectories on Qwen3.5-
0.8B and Qwen3.5-9B, covering seven benchmark families: MATH-500, MMLU-Pro, IFEval, GPQA Diamond,
MedXpertQA, SciBench, and SciCode [7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Our results reveal both the potential and the practical
challenges of WSP. On full held-out evaluations, successful interventions achieve substantial improvements across
multiple tasks, including a 29.45-percentage-point gain on MATH-500 from modifying only 1,023 parameter values.
We further find that a Math-edited checkpoint transfers to GPQA Diamond, IFEval, and SciBench, improving
accuracy by 3.03, 12.18, and 20.10 percentage points, respectively, without further editing. However, intervention
structures and outcomes vary considerably across trajectories, and six of the 16 trajectories ultimately retain the
original weights when candidate improvements cannot be reliably validated. Together, these findings demonstrate
that WSP can enable powerful and highly localized model adaptation, while highlighting the challenges of reliably
discovering effective interventions and predicting their effects across tasks. As shown in Figure 1, WSP yield
substantial held-out improvements across multiple benchmarks, particularly for the 0.8B model, while gains at the
9B scale are generally more modest.

Weight-Space Editing. Let θ0 denote the original model parameters and θt the current edited parameters. At
iteration t, the agent constructs an update ∆θt based on the current weights and accumulated observations Ht :

                                    θt+1 = bf16 (θt + ∆θt ) ,                  ∆θt = Et (θt , Ht ),                                (1)

where Et denotes the agent-generated editing program. Updates are computed in float32 and stored in bfloat16.
Edits are restricted to admissible transformer-block parameters, while token embeddings, final normalization, the
language-model head, and the model architecture remain fixed. The agent may construct arbitrary interventions
within these constraints, including sparse MLP modifications, rank-one updates, attention-head rescaling, and
combinations of these operations. All updates are computed directly from weights and observations rather than
gradient-based optimization.

Feedback and Evaluation. The agent evaluates candidate edits through two complementary modes: autoregres-
sive generation, which measures task performance, and teacher forcing, which provides token log-probabilities
and intermediate activations for mechanistic analysis. Based on development feedback, the agent selects a final
checkpoint, which is subsequently evaluated against the original model on held-out benchmarks under matched
evaluation settings.

3.4   Analysis of Weight-Space Interventions
Weight-Space Interventions Generalize to Unseen Questions. Our results demonstrate that weight-space
interventions can improve performance on previously unseen questions. Across three independent Math editing
trajectories, Qwen3.5-0.8B achieves held-out gains of +29.45, +5.00, and +27.30 percentage points. Task-specific
edits also improve GPQA Diamond (+4.42 pp), IFEval (+36.72 pp), and SciBench (+17.11 pp) on complete
held-out evaluations (Table 2). Together, these results establish that WSP improvements can extend beyond the
development data used to construct the interventions.

Editing Outcomes Vary Across Independent Searches.                The three independent MATH searches exhibit
substantial variation in their discovered interventions and held-out performance (Figure 4). Despite targeting the
same model and benchmark, the primary search achieves a +29.45 percentage-point improvement, while reruns 1
and 2 yield gains of +5.00 and +27.30 points, respectively. Although all three interventions modify the layer-23
MLP down-projection, they differ considerably in structure and parameter support. These results highlight the
path-dependent nature of weight-space exploration, where different search trajectories can discover markedly
different solutions within the same model.

Intervention Structure Matters Beyond Scale. Figure 5 reveals a striking contrast among three Math editing
trajectories. Although all three interventions target the layer-23 MLP down-projection, they occupy three distinct

Limited Search Budgets Constrain Weight Editing. Within the 50-request budget, Astra does not always
identify a sufficiently reliable weight intervention. For example, in 0.8B MedXpertQA, 9B GPQA Diamond, 9B
MedXpertQA, and 9B MMLU-Pro, the agent ultimately retains the original weights despite observing positive
development gains. For instance, the 9B GPQA search achieves an initial improvement of +6.25 percentage points,
but subsequent validation shows a slight performance decline. Similarly, the 9B MMLU-Pro search identifies
promising candidates whose improvements diminish under broader evaluation. These outcomes highlight a practical
limitation of WSP: within a fixed exploration budget, the agent may fail to discover and validate an intervention
sufficiently effective to justify modifying the original model.

Model Scale and Baseline Headroom Shape Editing Outcomes.                  Figure 7 reveals a consistent contrast
between baseline capability and observed editing gains across model scales. Qwen3.5-9B substantially outperforms
Qwen3.5-0.8B before editing, particularly on MATH-500 (97.70% versus 8.35%) and SciBench (72.46% versus
1.38%). In contrast, the 0.8B model achieves larger best-observed development gains across all seven benchmark
categories. This pattern highlights the role of initial model capability and available improvement headroom
in weight-space editing. The results suggest that smaller models offer greater opportunities for measurable
improvements, while stronger models present a more constrained search landscape.

4    Discussion
Our systematic evaluation reveals both the potential and limitations of Weight-Space Programming. Across diverse
tasks and model scales, WSP achieves substantial held-out improvements and exhibits asymmetric cross-task
transfer, with both beneficial and adverse effects. Independent editing trajectories further show that effectiveness
depends not only on the number of modified parameters, but also on intervention structure, computational targets,
and exploration paths. However, development gains do not always translate into reliable held-out improvements,
and repeated evaluations incur substantial computational costs. The mechanisms governing successful editing
and generalization remain unclear, including the effects of jointly modifying multiple parameter locations and
the influence of development data size and diversity. Moreover, WSP has yet to be systematically evaluated
on longer-horizon reasoning and complex agentic tasks. These findings highlight the need for more reliable
intervention selection, efficient evaluation, and deeper mechanistic understanding.

A     Supplementary Experimental Protocol
Each search pairs an editor model, GPT-6 Astra at maximum reasoning effort, with a target model, Qwen3.5-0.8B
or Qwen3.5-9B. The editor observes the target only through forward requests, with a budget of 50 successful
requests per search. It may also run up to 300 Python analyses per search to inspect weights, analyze returned
observations, and compute edits. These analyses do not run inference on the target model.
There are two request types, and each successful request of either type counts once against the budget. A generation
request (G) samples answers from the target and returns them with task scores when the items come from a
graded set. A teacher-forced request (TF) scores continuations supplied by the editor, returning conditional log-
probabilities and any requested activations. TF requests yield no task accuracy. The editor chooses the questions,
batch size, number of samples, token cap, and weight states for each request, and it may adapt these choices to
earlier observations. One request can therefore contain many prompts and samples, as well as both the original and
the edited weights.
Editing is gradient-free and acts directly on parameters. The editor uses no backward passes, no optimizers, and no
iterative loss fitting. Edits are computed in float32 and written to the BF16 checkpoint. Because of this, the number
of values an editor targets can be larger than the number of stored values that actually change. Table 3 lists the
settings shared by all searches.
The Math Primary and Math Rerun 2 searches used a broad ability-improvement objective from the start. Math
Rerun 1 began with a loop-focused objective that was broadened after 46 successful requests. The GPQA and
MedXpertQA objectives were broadened after 27 and 26 requests, respectively. These independent trajectories vary
in prompt history and in the editor’s adaptive experimental choices.
