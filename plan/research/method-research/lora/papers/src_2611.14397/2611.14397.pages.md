                                                                                            Abstract

                                                   Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) is the de facto method for parameter-efficient fine-
                                                   tuning of large neural networks, achieving strong task adaptation with minimal
                                                   trainable parameters and low optimization cost. Beyond this efficiency, practition-
                                                   ers have observed two striking properties: robustness to catastrophic forgetting and
                                                   the ability to merge independently trained adapters into a single model that per-
                                                   forms competitively across multiple tasks. These properties are central to continual
                                                   and multi-task learning, yet remain poorly understood. In this work, we provide a
                                                   theoretical explanation through a unified margin-based perspective. We analyze
                                                   LoRA in multiclass linear classification under a near-orthogonal task regime with
                                                   ℓ2 -regularization, and characterize the optimal LoRA adapter across regularization
                                                   regimes. Our analysis shows that, in an intermediate regime of regularization pa-
                                                   rameters, the optimal adapter aligns with the max-margin solution on the fine-tuning
                                                   data. Building on this characterization, we derive two key consequences. First,
                                                   we obtain closed-form expressions for the margins on pre-training and fine-tuning
                                                   data, revealing a precise margin trade-off: the regularization parameter controls the
                                                   balance between retention and adaptation. Second, we analyze adapter merging,
                                                   proving that merged models achieve positive margin on each task and deriving
                                                   optimal mixing coefficients that balance margins across tasks and maximize the
                                                   margin over their union. These results lead to a simple, training-free merging rule,
                                                   which we term margin-based LoRA merging (MaLoRA). Experiments on modern
                                                   architectures and real datasets validate our theoretical predictions, showing that
                                                   MaLoRA matches or outperforms several adapter-merging baselines across a range
                                                   of vision and language classification tasks.


                                         1     Introduction
                                         Foundation models have transformed artificial intelligence across numerous domains. However,
                                         deploying these models in real-world applications typically requires fine-tuning on specialized
                                         datasets to meet accuracy, safety, and alignment requirements. Traditional full fine-tuning, which
                                         updates all parameters of a pre-trained model, incurs substantial computational cost, including large
                                         memory footprints, long training times, and significant storage demands. Low-Rank Adaptation
                                         (LoRA) [Hu et al., 2022] addresses this challenge by augmenting pre-trained weight matrices W with
                                         low-rank adapters B, A while keeping W frozen. Empirically, LoRA matches the performance of
                                         full fine-tuning while training only a small fraction of the parameters.

While LoRA was originally introduced for its computational efficiency, practitioners have since
identified two striking empirical properties.

1. Robustness to catastrophic forgetting. In fine-tuning and continual learning, the degradation of
   performance on previously learned tasks after adapting to new ones (a.k.a. catastrophic forgetting)
   is a fundamental challenge [McCloskey and Cohen, 1989, Kirkpatrick et al., 2017, Ramasesh et al.,
   2021]. Surprisingly, recent studies show that LoRA exhibits strong robustness to catastrophic
   forgetting, even after substantial adaptation [Wistuba et al., 2023, Biderman et al., 2024, Qiao and
   Mahdavi, 2024]. This robustness makes LoRA particularly appealing for continual learning.
2. Effective adapter merging. In multi-task settings, maintaining separate fine-tuned models for each
   task is storage-intensive. Remarkably, LoRA adapters trained independently on different tasks can
   be merged (e.g., via simple weighted averaging) into a single adapter that performs well across all
   tasks [Huang et al., 2023, Yadav et al., 2023, Wang et al., 2024, Yu et al., 2024, Zhao et al., 2024].
   This property enables flexible and efficient deployment.

Despite these empirical benefits, the theoretical mechanisms underlying LoRA’s robustness to
catastrophic forgetting and its adapter merging capabilities remain poorly understood. In this work,
we provide a unified theoretical explanation for both phenomena through a margin-based perspective.
Paper contributions. We study LoRA in multiclass linear classification under a near-orthogonal-task
regime, in which fine-tuning samples are nearly orthogonal to one another and to the pre-training
samples. In this setting, we characterize the global minimizer of the LoRA objective and use this
characterization to analyze forgetting and adapter merging. Compared with prior theoretical work on
LoRA (see Appendix B), our work makes the following contributions:

• Characterization of the optimal solution. We characterize the global minimizer of LoRA across
  different regularization regimes (Theorem 3.1). In the high-penalty regime, the optimal adapter
  is zero. In the intermediate regime, the adapter aligns with the max-margin direction on the
  fine-tuning data. In the low-penalty regime, the adapter aligns with a non-max-margin classifier
  whose direction generally does not admit a simple closed form. Nevertheless, we show that this
  direction converges to an interpretable limit as the regularization parameter tends to zero.
• Theory for reduced forgetting. We define forgetting as the reduction in margin on the pre-training
  data after fine-tuning, and derive closed-form expressions for the normalized margins on the pre-
  training data, the fine-tuning data, and their union (Theorem 3.2). Our analysis shows that these
  margins are governed by the regularization parameter and satisfy a precise trade-off: decreasing the
  regularization parameter increases the fine-tuning margin but also increases the Frobenius-norm
  ratio of the adapter to the pre-trained weights, leading to greater forgetting. We further identify the
  regularization parameter that maximizes the margin on the union of pre-training and fine-tuning
  tasks, thereby optimally balancing retention and adaptation.
• Theory for adapter merging. Under the intermediate-regime assumption for each task, we derive
  closed-form expressions for the margin of the merged model on every task (Theorem 3.3). We
  show that the merged model attains strictly positive margin on each task, providing a theoretical
  explanation for the effectiveness of adapter merging. We also characterize, in closed form, the
  mixing coefficients that maximize the margin of the merged model on the union of all tasks, which
  naturally leads to margin-based LoRA merging (MaLoRA), a theoretically motivated adapter-
  merging method. This contrasts with many existing model-merging approaches, which rely
  primarily on heuristic prescriptions for choosing how to combine task-specific models.
• Numerical validation. Experiments on real datasets and modern architectures support the theory
  in three ways: (i) in the linear probing setting, the empirically optimal regularization parameter
  agrees with the value predicted by our analysis; (ii) in the same setting, the empirically optimal
  adapter mixing weights agree with our closed-form coefficients; and (iii) in LoRA adapter merging,
  MaLoRA achieves performance comparable to, and in several cases better than, strong heuristic
  adapter-merging baselines across vision and language classification tasks.

2.2   Pre-training and fine-tuning setup

We now describe the specific pre-training and fine-tuning framework used in our theoretical analysis.
Pre-training stage. Let Dpre be a labeled dataset with K classes, and let Wpre ∈ RK×d denote a
pre-trained linear classifier obtained by GD minimizing the cross-entropy loss L(W ; Dpre ). When
Dpre is linearly separable, this objective admits no finite minimizer. The GD iterates instead diverge
in norm while converging in direction to the max-margin solution [Lyu and Li, 2019, Soudry et al.,
2018]. We formalize this directional structure in Assumption 2.4.
Fine-tuning stage. We adapt the pre-trained model to a downstream dataset Dft consisting of n
additional samples from each of 2 ≤ K̄ ≤ K classes selected from the original K pre-training classes.
This restriction applies throughout the paper and appendix, including to every task in adapter merging.
This setup captures practical scenarios such as domain-incremental learning [Esaki et al., 2024],
domain adaptation [Zohrizadeh et al., 2019, Zhang et al., 2022], and bias-rebalancing fine-tuning [Li
and Xu, 2021, ValizadehAslani et al., 2024].
When LoRA is applied to adapt the pre-trained model to the fine-tuning task, we introduce a low-rank
update to the weights, parameterized by matrices B ∈ RK×r and A ∈ Rr×d :
                             minB,A L(Wpre + BA; Dft ) + λ2 (∥B∥2F + ∥A∥2F ).                                               (5)
Here, the Frobenius-norm penalties explicitly constrain the adapters in weight space, limiting the
deviation of the fine-tuned model from the pretrained initialization.

3     Main Results

In this section, we first analyze the optimization landscape of the LoRA objective and characterize
its global minimum in §3.1. We then derive closed-form expressions for the margins on the union
of pre-training and fine-tuning datasets, explaining why LoRA mitigates catastrophic forgetting in
§3.2. Finally, in §3.3, we extend our margin-based analysis to the multi-task setting, explaining the
effectiveness of adapter merging and deriving optimal mixing coefficients that maximize the margin
on the union of all tasks. Moreover, building on these closed-form coefficients, we translate them
into a simple training-free merging rule, margin-based LoRA merging (MaLoRA, Algorithm 1).

Theorem 3.3 characterizes the margins on each task as well as on the union of tasks as functions of
the mixing coefficients. In particular, it shows that the optimal mixing coefficients are given by the
ratio between the unnormalized margin of the pre-trained model on Dpre and that of each fine-tuned
adapter on its own task. The unnormalized margin measures how confidently a classifier separates
classes on its dataset, with smaller values indicating more challenging classification problems. This
formula admits a direct interpretation: an adapter that achieves a smaller margin on its task is assigned
a larger mixing weight, increasing its contribution so as to preserve a positive margin for that task in
the merged model.

Towards practical margin-based adapter mixing The preceding analysis suggests choosing
adapter mixing coefficients according to how confidently each adapter separates its task data, as
measured by the unnormalized margin. In practice, however, datasets are often not perfectly separable,
so the minimum margin C(·; ·) can be negative and overly sensitive to hard examples. We therefore
use the average margin C̄(·; D) as a more stable proxy for adapter performance. Under the strict-
orthogonal assumptions of Theorem 3.3, the per-sample margins of each optimal adapter are equal
on its own fine-tuning dataset. Consequently, minimum and average margins coincide on these
fine-tuning datasets. In our practical algorithm, we therefore use average margins as empirical proxies
for the minimum margins appearing in the theoretical mixing rule.

4     Experiments

Our experiments aim to validate the theoretical predictions in Section 3 and assess how well they
transfer beyond the linear setting, rather than to establish state-of-the-art performance. Since our
analysis gives closed-form predictions for optimal regularization and adapter mixing coefficients, we
test whether these quantities match empirical behavior in modern architectures. Specifically, we focus
on two questions: (i) whether the mixing coefficients derived from our margin analysis agree with
empirically optimal coefficients, and (ii) whether the resulting theory-driven merging rule, MaLoRA,
remains competitive with adapter-merging baselines in nonlinear models. Experiments on the effect
of regularization are deferred to Appendix H.1.2.
Setup and metric. We evaluate our theory and method in two complementary settings. First,
in linear probing on CIFAR-100 [Krizhevsky et al., 2009] (§4.1), we use four frozen pre-trained
backbones (ResNet-50, ViT-B/16, ConvNeXt, and CLIP) as feature extractors [He et al., 2016,
Dosovitskiy et al., 2021, Radford et al., 2021, Liu et al., 2022], apply LoRA only to the final linear
classifier [Kornblith et al., 2019], and construct tasks from the CIFAR-100 superclass hierarchy.
Second, in multi-domain adapter merging (§4.2), we evaluate ViT-B/16 on PACS, VLCS, and
TerraIncognita [Fang et al., 2013, Li et al., 2017, Beery et al., 2018], and XLM-RoBERTa Base on
MNLI, Amazon Reviews, and CivilComments [Zhang et al., 2015, Williams et al., 2018, Borkan
et al., 2019, Conneau et al., 2020]. Although our theory is stated in terms of normalized margin,
we report classification accuracy in experiments, since real-world classification tasks often do not
achieve perfect separation and can have negative normalized margins. Additional details are given in
Appendix H.

5   Conclusion, Limitations, and Future Work
In this work, we study LoRA fine-tuning through the normalized margin under a near-orthogonal
task regime. We characterize the global minimizer of the regularized LoRA objective across three
regularization regimes (Theorem 3.1), derive closed-form expressions for the margins on the pre-
training and fine-tuning data, and identify the regularization level that maximizes the margin on their
union (Theorem 3.2). We further extend the analysis to multi-task settings by deriving closed-form
optimal mixing coefficients for adapter merging (Theorem 3.3). Based on this theory, we propose
margin-based LoRA merging (MaLoRA, Algorithm 1), which matches or outperforms several adapter-
merging baselines on a range of vision and language benchmarks. Across architectures and tasks, the
empirical results closely align with the theoretical predictions.
Limitations and future work. First, our analysis relies on simplifying assumptions, most notably
near-orthogonal task structure (Assumption 2.1) and balanced class sizes in the fine-tuning data.
These assumptions enable a sharp closed-form characterization, but they also limit the scope of the
theory. An important direction for future work is to relax them and study LoRA under more general
data distributions and pre-training conditions. Second, experiments applying MaLoRA to nonlinear
models provide empirical support for the practical usefulness of our merging rule beyond multiclass
linear classification. These findings motivate extending the margin-based theoretical analysis to
nonlinear adaptation. Third, our theory characterizes margins on the training datasets; establishing
corresponding generalization or test-risk guarantees requires further analysis.

B    Related work


Theory of LoRA. There are many works theoretically studying the expressiveness, characterizing
the loss landscape, and understanding the learning dynamics of LoRA. Zeng and Lee [2023] prove
that, under mild assumptions, LoRA can approximate any deep linear, feed-forward, or transformer
network. Within the NTK regime, Malladi et al. [2023] characterize the conditions under which
one can
      √ study LoRA in the NTK regime, while Jang et al. [2024] show that when the LoRA rank is
ri ≳ N , where N is the number of√samples, the optimization landscape of LoRA has no spurious
local minima, and GD can find O( N )-rank solutions that generalize well. Moreover, Xu et al.
[2025] study the learning dynamics of LoRA in the context of matrix factorization, and show that
smaller initialization leads to longer training time and lower training error. However, none of these
studies theoretically characterize why LoRA reduces catastrophic forgetting and support effective
adapter merging.
LoRA merging. A variety of techniques have been proposed to merge task-specific LoRA adapters by
forming a weighted average of their parameters. For example, LoRA-Hub [Huang et al., 2023] learns
per-task mixing coefficients first, and then applies a weighted average of LoRA weight matrices B
and A separately. LoRA-Flow [Wang et al., 2024] introduces token-level gates that dynamically assign
merging coefficients to each LoRA adapter before taking weighted averaging. LoRA-Retriever [Zhao
et al., 2024] first retrieves the most relevant adapters for each input, and then averages the selected
LoRA adapters. Beyond weighted averaging, another line of work attempts to address redundancy and
conflicting updates directly. TIES [Yadav et al., 2023] proceeds in three steps: trimming redundant
parameters, resolving sign conflicts into an aggregate vector, and averaging only the parameters
consistent with the aggregate sign, thereby mitigating degradation from redundant or conflicting
updates. DARE [Yu et al., 2024] can be used as a preprocessing step for other merging methods,
where parameters are randomly dropped according to a specified rate and the remaining ones are
rescaled, reducing redundancy and potential interference among merged adapters. However, all these
methods remain largely heuristic and lack theoretical guarantees.
Regularization and task orthogonality help reduce forgetting. A large body of work in fine-
tuning and continual learning shows that controlling deviation of the fine-tuned model from the
pretrained model reduces catastrophic forgetting. Concretely, these approaches impose weight-space
penalties that restrict the magnitude and direction of parameter updates away from the pretrained
solution. Weight-anchoring and importance-aware penalties exemplify this idea: Kirkpatrick et al.
[2017] penalize movement along directions identified by the Fisher information matrix, while Zenke
et al. [2017] accumulate path-wise importance during training. Schwarz et al. [2018] extend this
idea to long sequences of tasks. Related approaches estimate parameter sensitivity [Aljundi et al.,
2018], apply output-level distillation to preserve prior behavior [Li and Hoiem, 2016], or explicitly
regularize parameters toward the pretrained anchor [Li et al., 2018]. Although these approaches
differ in how importance or anchoring is computed, the common mechanism is the same: weight-
space regularizers constrain updates during fine-tuning relative to the pretrained model to mitigate
catastrophic forgetting. Another complementary perspective highlights the role of task relations.
When task representations are sufficiently decorrelated, interference between tasks is naturally
reduced and forgetting is alleviated. In linear and kernelized models, it has been shown that when
tasks are orthogonal or nearly orthogonal, cross-task gradient interference vanishes in expectation,
implying negligible forgetting [Doan et al., 2021, Evron et al., 2022]. These insights resonate with
our analysis of LoRA, where we explicitly study a regularized objective on low-rank adapters and
make the simplifying assumption that the fine-tuning data are orthogonal to the pretraining data. This
assumption directly connects to prior findings on task orthogonality and provides a tractable setting
in which we can isolate and analyze the role of regularization in mitigating forgetting.
